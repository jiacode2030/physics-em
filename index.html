    Charge slices

    When charge is spread along a rod, over a ring or through a ball, you can't plug it into kq/r². Cut it into tiny slices that are each small enough to count as point charges, then add them up. That sum is an integral.

    The intuition

    The recipe is always the same: cut, treat each piece as a point charge, add. A tiny piece of charge is called dq. For a rod, dq = λ dx, where λ is the charge per meter. A slice of a surface has dq = σ dA, and a slice of a volume has dq = ρ dV.

    Slide the number of slices up in the simulation. With one slice the answer is a guess. With ten it is close. With forty it matches the exact integral to within a fraction of a percent. An integral is what you get when the slices become infinitely thin.

    Two tricks save most of the work: use symmetry to cancel components before you integrate, and choose slices that are all the same distance from the point (a thin ring, for a point on its axis).

    dE = k dq/r²field of one slice
    E = ∫ dEadd them all (as vectors)

    Rod total charge is 10 nC. Each slice makes a small field pointing left (away from the rod, since the charge is positive). Add the arrows and the total approaches the exact answer. Putting all the charge at one point is only a rough shortcut.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Use one slice, then add more. Watch the error shrink.

      What you should see

      One slice puts all the charge at the middle of the rod, which is just a point-charge guess. Increase the slices and the sum climbs toward the exact value.

    2. Move the point far from the rod (a = 0.5 m).

      What you should see

      Far away the rod looks like a point charge. Even three slices are close, and the "everything at the center" shortcut is within about 10%.

    3. Bring the point close to the rod (a = 0.05 m) with 3 slices.

      What you should see

      Up close, the near end matters most because of the 1/r². Few slices miss that, and the "everything at the center" shortcut is far off.

    4. Switch to the ramp: charge grows toward the far end.

      What you should see

      Most of the charge is far from P, so the field is smaller than for the same total charge spread evenly.

    Worked example

    A rod lies along the x-axis from x = 0 to L = 0.40 m. Its charge density grows along the rod: λ = bx with b = 5.0 μC/m². Find the total charge, and the electric field at point P on the axis, a = 0.10 m to the left of the rod.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Pick a slice. dq = λ dx = bx dx. It is a distance a + x from P.
    2. Total charge. Q = ∫0L bx dx = bL²/2 = (5.0 × 10⁻⁶)(0.40)²/2 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ C = 0.40 μC.
    3. Field of one slice. dE = k dq/(a + x)² = kb x dx/(a + x)², pointing away from the rod (−x), because the charge is positive. All slices point the same way, so the sizes just add.
    4. Integrate. E = kb ∫0L x dx/(a + x)² = kb [ln((a + L)/a) + a/(a + L) − 1]. (Use x = (a + x) − a to split the integrand.)
    5. Numbers. ln(5) + 0.20 − 1 = 0.809, so E = (8.99 × 10⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁶)(0.809) = 3.6 × 10⁴ N/C, directed in the −x direction.
    6. Sanity check. A point charge of 0.40 μC at the rod's charge-weighted center (0.267 m from the end, 0.367 m from P) would give 2.7 × 10⁴ N/C. The real answer is larger because 1/r² weights the nearer charge more heavily than the average position suggests. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Using a single r for all the slices when they are at different distances.
    • Forgetting the integrand needs x written in terms of the variable (dq = λ dx with λ depending on x).
    • Adding field magnitudes when the slices' arrows point in different directions (rings and arcs). Use symmetry or components.
    • Integrating over the wrong limits: the slice position runs along the object, not from P.

    Exponential change

    Charging capacitors, decaying currents in coils, cooling coffee and falling raindrops all follow the same curve: it changes fastest at first, then eases into its final value. One number, the time constant τ, sets the whole timeline.

    The intuition

    Fill a bathtub through a leaky tap that slows as the tub fills. At first the water rises quickly; as the tub fills the flow slows, until it barely changes. Rule: the rate of change is proportional to how far you still have to go. That sentence, written as an equation, gives the exponential.

    The time constant τ is how long it would take if the initial rate kept going. A bigger τ means a slower process. After one τ the quantity is 63% of the way there; after two, 86%; after three, 95%; after five it is within 1%. In practice, "done" means about 5τ.

    You will meet this curve in RC circuits (τ = RC), in LR circuits (τ = L/R), and in Mechanics for drag. It is the same differential equation each time, only the letters change.

    f(t) = 1 − e−t/τfills up to its final value
    f(t) = e−t/τdies away to zero
    df/dt = (1 − f)/τrate ∝ distance still to go

    The curve changes fastest at the start, and each τ takes it a fixed fraction of the way from where it is to where it's going. It never quite arrives.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Slide time to exactly one time constant (t = τ).

      What you should see

      The curve is at 63% of the way. That is 1 − 1/e. Try another τ: whenever t = τ it is still 63%.

    2. Make τ larger (slow process), keep t the same.

      What you should see

      A bigger τ stretches the curve out in time. At the same t you are much less far along.

    3. Switch to the dying curve and read it at 2τ and 3τ.

      What you should see

      At t = 2τ only 14% is left; at 3τ, 5%. The remaining amount shrinks by the same factor, 1/e, every time τ passes.

    4. How long until the fill-up curve is within 10% of the end?

      What you should see

      About 2.3τ. You can read it off the "Time to get within 10%" readout. Within 1% takes 4.6τ.

    Worked example

    After you take a flash photo, the camera recharges its flash capacitor. Model the recharge as V(t) = 300(1 − e^(−t/τ)) volts with τ = 1.5 s. Find the voltage 2.0 s after the shot, and how long it takes until the "flash ready" light comes on at 280 V.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Check what the curve should do. It starts at 0 (the flash just emptied the capacitor), rises quickly at first, and approaches 300 V without crossing it.
    2. Voltage at 2.0 s. t/τ = 2.0/1.5 = 1.33, so V = 300(1 − e−1.33) = 300(1 − 0.264) = 221 V.
    3. Time to reach 280 V. Set 280 = 300(1 − e−t/τ), so e−t/τ = 1 − 280/300 = 1/15.
    4. Solve for t (take the natural log). t = τ ln 15 = (1.5 s)(2.71) = 4.1 s.
    5. Sanity check. 280 V is 93% of the final value: between 2τ = 3.0 s (86%) and 3τ = 4.5 s (95%) ✓. That few-second wait is the pause you notice between flash shots.

    Common mistakes

    • Reading the time constant as the time to finish. It is the time to reach 63%, not 100%.
    • Mixing up charging and decaying forms: one has 1 − e^(−t/τ), the other e^(−t/τ).
    • Forgetting the minus sign in the exponent (the curve would blow up).
    • Entering t in different units from τ. Keep both in seconds.

    Where the curve comes from

    • If the rate is proportional to what's left, df/dt = (1 − f)/τ, separate variables: df/(1 − f) = dt/τ. Integrate: −ln(1 − f) = t/τ. Solve: f = 1 − e−t/τ.
    • Slope at the start is 1/τ. If the curve kept that slope it would reach the end at t = τ. This tangent line is a handy way to read τ off a graph.
    • Handy numbers: 1 − e⁻¹ = 0.632, 1 − e⁻² = 0.865, 1 − e⁻³ = 0.950, 1 − e⁻⁴ = 0.982, 1 − e⁻⁵ = 0.993. Half-way takes 0.693τ.

    Solving "how long until…"

    • Isolate the exponential, take the natural log of both sides, and multiply by τ: t = −τ ln(1 − f) for the fill-up, t = −τ ln f for the decay.

    Charge & Coulomb's law

    Charge comes in two kinds. Like charges push apart, opposite charges pull together, and the push or pull falls off with the square of the distance, exactly like gravity does, but trillions of times stronger.

    The intuition

    Rub a balloon on your hair and it sticks to the wall. Rubbing moved electrons from one surface to the other, so one surface is left slightly negative and the other slightly positive. Charge is never created or destroyed, only moved. Every object is made of huge numbers of + and − charges that almost perfectly cancel.

    Coulomb's law says how hard two small charges push or pull: double one charge and the force doubles; double the distance and the force drops to a quarter. It has the same shape as Newton's gravity law, with charge playing the role of mass. The big difference is that gravity only attracts, while electric forces can attract or repel, and they are enormously stronger: the electric force between a proton and an electron in hydrogen is about 10³⁹ times the gravitational pull between them.

    When there are three or more charges, the force on any one is found by superposition: work out the force from each of the others as if it were alone, then add the arrows as vectors.

    2. Drag charge 2 to about twice as far from charge 1 as it was. Read the force before and after.

      What you should see

      Double the distance and the force drops to one quarter. The readout "Force if r doubled" predicts it before you move.

    3. Switch to three charges. Make charges 2 and 3 equal and place charge 1 midway between them.

      What you should see

      Equal pushes from opposite sides cancel, so the net force on charge 1 is zero. Superposition works on arrows, not just numbers.

    4. Double the size of charge 2 (−2 μC to −4 μC) and compare the force readout.

      What you should see

      Doubling either charge doubles the force. The force is proportional to the product q₁q₂.

    Worked example

    A +2.0 μC charge sits at x = 0, a −3.0 μC charge at x = 0.20 m and a +4.0 μC charge at x = 0.40 m. Find the net electric force on the −3.0 μC charge.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Draw the arrows first. The −3.0 μC charge is attracted to both positive charges. The +2.0 μC charge is to its left, so that force points left (−x). The +4.0 μC charge is to its right, so that force points right (+x).
    2. Force from the +2.0 μC charge (r = 0.20 m). F1 = (8.99 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)(3.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.20)² = 1.35 N, pointing −x.
    3. Force from the +4.0 μC charge (r = 0.20 m). F3 = (8.99 × 10⁹)(4.0 × 10⁻⁶)(3.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.20)² = 2.70 N, pointing +x.
    4. Add the vectors. Fnet = +2.70 − 1.35 = +1.35 N, so the net force is 1.35 N in the +x direction.
    5. Check. The stronger pull comes from the bigger charge, which is on the right, so the net force points right. Both charges are the same distance away, so the net is just k(3.0 μC)(4.0 − 2.0 μC)/(0.20)² = 1.35 N ✓.

    Common mistakes

    • Using the signs of the charges in the formula and choosing the direction from the picture, which counts the sign twice. Use |q₁q₂| for the size and the sketch for the direction.
    • Adding force magnitudes when the forces point in different directions. Add components.
    • Putting charge in microcoulombs or distance in centimeters straight into the formula. Convert to C and m.
    • Forgetting that the force is on one charge from the others. A charge never feels a force from itself.

    What the law says

    • Direction: along the line joining the charges. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
    • Size: proportional to each charge and to 1/r². Double the distance, a quarter of the force; triple it, a ninth.
    • Newton's third law: the force on charge 1 from charge 2 is equal and opposite to the force on 2 from 1, even if the charges are very different.
    • Charge is quantized: it comes in whole multiples of e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. One electron carries −e, one proton +e. A coulomb is a huge charge: about 6 × 10¹⁸ electrons.
    • Conservation of charge: the total never changes. If two identical conducting spheres touch, they share charge equally.

    Conductors and insulators

    • Conductors (metals) let charge move freely; insulators (rubber, glass) hold it in place.
    • A charged rod can pull on a neutral object by polarization: charges inside shift slightly so that the nearer charge, which is opposite, attracts more than the farther one repels.

    Using it with gravity-sized numbers

    • Electric forces dominate for atoms and molecules; gravity dominates for planets only because large objects are almost perfectly neutral.
    • The same 1/r² shape means a lot of what you learned about gravity carries over, including adding forces as vectors and the idea of a field.

    Electric fields

    A charge changes the space around it. Put another charge anywhere in that space and it feels a push or pull. The electric field is a map of that push, drawn before the second charge even arrives.

    The intuition

    Think of a weather map with wind arrows. You don't need to throw a leaf to know which way it would blow; the arrows already tell you. The electric field is the same kind of map: at every point it shows the force a tiny positive test charge would feel, per coulomb of its charge.

    Positive charges push the test charge away, so their field points outward. Negative charges pull it in, so their field points inward. With several charges, work out each one's arrow and add them, tip to tail. That's all superposition means.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Start with one positive charge, then flip it negative.

      What you should see

      The lines keep the same shape but now point inward. The probe's arrow turns around and points at the charge.

    2. Make a dipole and put the probe exactly between the charges.

      What you should see

      The field points from + toward −. Both charges' arrows point the same way there, so they add: the field between a dipole's charges is strong.

    3. Two equal positive charges: find the spot where the field is zero.

      What you should see

      Exactly halfway between them the two pushes are equal and opposite, so |E| drops to zero. No field lines pass through that point.

    4. Make charge 2 twice as big as charge 1, both positive.

      What you should see

      Twice as many lines leave charge 2. The zero-field point moves toward the smaller charge, where the weaker charge's field can still match the stronger one's.

    Worked example

    A +4.0 nC charge sits at x = 0 and a −2.0 nC charge sits at x = 0.30 m. Find the electric field at x = 0.10 m, and the force on an electron placed there.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Draw the arrows first. The + charge pushes a test charge away from it: to the right. The − charge pulls a test charge toward it: also to the right. Same direction, so the magnitudes add.
    2. Field from the +4.0 nC charge (r = 0.10 m). E1 = (8.99 × 10⁹)(4.0 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.10)² = 3.60 × 10³ N/C, pointing +x.
    3. Field from the −2.0 nC charge (r = 0.20 m). E2 = (8.99 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.20)² = 4.5 × 10² N/C, pointing +x.
    4. Add. E = 3.60 × 10³ + 0.45 × 10³ = 4.05 × 10³ N/C in the +x direction.
    5. Force on the electron. F = qE = (−1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)(4.05 × 10³) = −6.5 × 10⁻¹⁶ N: magnitude 6.5 × 10⁻¹⁶ N, pointing −x. A negative charge is pushed opposite to E.

    Common mistakes

    • Putting the sign of the charge into kq/r² and then also choosing a direction from a picture. Use magnitudes for size, the picture for direction.
    • Adding field magnitudes when the arrows point different ways. Add components.
    • Thinking E at a point depends on the charge you put there. E is set by the other charges; the charge you place only decides the force, F = qE.
    • Forgetting that E points opposite to the force on a negative charge.

    Reading field lines

    • Direction: at every point the field is tangent to the line through it. Lines start on positive charges and end on negative ones (or run off to infinity).
    • Strength: where lines crowd together the field is strong; where they spread out it is weak. The number of lines leaving a charge is proportional to its charge.
    • Lines never cross. The field has one direction at each point, so two lines can't pass through the same spot.
    • A charge is not pushed along a field line forever. The line gives the direction of the force, not the path. A moving charge keeps its momentum and curves away from the line.

    The equations behind the picture

    • Point charge: E = kq r̂/r², where r̂ points from the charge to the point. With k = 1/(4πε0) and ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²).
    • Superposition: E = Σ kqi r̂i/ri². Split each arrow into x and y components, add each column, then recombine.
    • Dipole: far away (r much bigger than the separation d), its field falls off as 1/r³, faster than a single charge, because the + and − nearly cancel.
    • Units: N/C. You will also see V/m; they are the same unit.

    Rods, rings & disks

    Real charged objects aren't points. Slice a ring or a disk into tiny pieces, let symmetry cancel the sideways parts, and the sum gives the field along the axis. Far away it becomes kQ/z² again, and up close a disk becomes an infinite sheet.

    The intuition

    Take a ring of charge and a point on its axis. Every slice of the ring is the same distance from the point, so the integral is easy. But the slices pull in different directions: each field points away from its slice, partly along the axis and partly sideways. The sideways parts of opposite slices cancel, leaving only the axial part.

The result starts at zero in the center, grows rapidly as you approach, and levels off at σ/2ε₀ right at the surface: exactly the field of an infinite sheet, because to a point very close to it, the disk looks endless.

    • Infinite sheet: let R → ∞ and the bracket becomes 1: E = σ/2ε0, the same at every distance. It points away from the sheet on both sides.

    • For a point much farther than the object's size, treat it as a point charge: kQ/z².
    • For a point much closer than the object's size, treat it as an infinite plane or line.

    Flux & Gauss's law

    Gauss's law is Coulomb's law in a more powerful costume. Draw an imaginary closed surface, count the net field passing through it, and the answer depends only on the charge inside. When the charge has symmetry, it gives the field in a single line.

    The intuition

    Imagine field lines as water streaming out of every positive charge. Put a closed net around some charges and count the water flowing through it. The count only depends on how many positive sources are inside, minus how many negative sinks. Move the charges around inside, change the shape of the net, even add charges outside: the net count doesn't change.

    That count is the electric flux, Φ. Gauss's law says Φ equals the enclosed charge divided by ε₀. In the simulation the "measured flux" adds up E·n̂ over the whole sphere; it always matches qenc/ε₀, however the charges are arranged.

    The law is always true. It is only useful for finding E when the field is the same everywhere on your surface (or zero, or parallel to it). That needs symmetry: spherical, cylindrical or planar. Then ∮E·dA becomes just E times an area, and you can solve for E.

    Common mistakes

    • Using Gauss's law to find E when there is no symmetry. The law still holds, but you can't pull E out of the integral.
    • Counting charge outside the surface. Only enclosed charge appears in the law (the outside charges do affect E, but their flux cancels).
    • For a non-conducting sphere, using the full Q inside the sphere instead of the fraction Q(r/R)³.
    • Thinking E is zero everywhere when the flux is zero. Zero net flux just means as much field leaves as enters.

    What flux means

    • For a flat surface in a uniform field, Φ = EA cos θ, where θ is the angle between E and the surface's normal (the line sticking straight out of it). Face-on: maximum. Edge-on: zero.
    • For a curved surface or a non-uniform field, chop the surface into little patches, add up E · dA for each, and the sum is the integral ∮ E · dA. The circle on the integral means the surface is closed.
    • Outward flux counts positive, inward flux negative.

    Electric potential

    Voltage is the energy-per-charge version of the electric field. Instead of arrows to add, you get one number at each point, like the height on a hill. Positive charges slide downhill, negative charges slide uphill, and equal-height lines are called equipotentials.

    The intuition

    A hiker on a hill feels a force along the steepest downhill direction. Electric potential V works the same way: the field E points downhill on the V map, and its size is the steepness. In symbols, E = −dV/dx. A contour line on a hill map is the line of equal height; an equipotential is a line of equal V, and field lines always cross it at a right angle.

    Potential is defined as energy per charge: V = U/q. A 1 V potential difference means every coulomb gains 1 joule when it falls through it. A battery is a device that holds a potential difference between its terminals.

    Since V is a number, not an arrow, adding potentials from several charges is just addition, with signs. That makes potential much easier than field for many-charge problems. Find V, then take a slope if you need E.

    Common mistakes

    • Adding potentials as vectors, or using the vector sum of fields. V is a scalar.
    • Dropping signs: a negative charge contributes a negative number to V.
    • Saying E = 0 wherever V = 0 (or V = 0 wherever E = 0). They are different things: E is the slope of V, not its value.
    • Mixing up energy and potential: U = qV. A 1 V difference gives an electron 1 eV of energy, not 1 J.

    Potential and field

    • E points downhill (toward lower V), perpendicular to equipotentials. Where contour lines crowd together, V changes fast and the field is strong.
    • In one dimension: Ex = −dV/dx. In 3D, E = −∇V (the three partial derivatives).
    • Uniform field: V changes linearly: ΔV = −Ed over a distance d along E. The potential is higher on the side the field points away from.
    • Zero field does not mean zero potential. Inside a conductor E = 0 but V is the same nonzero constant everywhere.

    Why equipotentials matter

    • No work is done moving a charge along an equipotential (ΔV = 0).
    • A conductor in equilibrium is an equipotential, so field lines hit its surface at right angles.
    • The potential of a point charge is kq/r, not 1/r²: only one power of r in the denominator.

    Potential of a continuous charge distribution

    • Because V is a scalar, the integral is easier than for E: V = ∫ k dq/r, with no components and no cancelling. Slice the charge, find each slice's distance to the point, add.
    • Ring, on its axis: every slice is the same distance, so V = kQ/√(z² + R²).
    • Disk, on its axis: stack rings: V = (σ/2ε0)(√(z² + R²) − z).
    • Spherical shell: outside, kQ/r; inside, the potential is constant, kQ/R (E = 0 inside, so V cannot change).
    • Solid uniform sphere: outside kQ/r; inside kQ(3R² − r²)/(2R³), so the center is at 3kQ/(2R).
    • From E to V: V(b) − V(a) = −∫ab E · dr. Work inward from the zero of potential (usually infinity) and integrate piece by piece when E changes form inside and outside an object.

    Energy of charges

    Move a charge through a potential difference and it gains or loses energy: ΔU = qΔV. That one line gives the speed of particles in accelerators, TV tubes and mass spectrometers, and it gives the energy stored in any arrangement of charges.

    The intuition

    Think of a charge as a ball on the potential hill. Let it fall through a voltage ΔV and it gains kinetic energy qΔV; the potential energy it loses becomes motion. No need to know the field or the path in between: only the voltage difference matters. That is why this is the tool for "speed after being accelerated through…" questions.

    Physicists often use a new unit of energy: the electron-volt. One eV is the energy an electron (or any single-charge particle) gains falling through 1 V: 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. A proton accelerated through 500 V gains 500 eV, with no multiplication needed.

    For several charges, the energy of the arrangement is the work it took to assemble them: add up kq₁q₂/r for every pair. Positive means you had to push (like charges close together); negative means the system is bound.

    Conductors

    In a metal, charges can move freely. So when the field is not zero inside, charges keep moving until it is. That single fact explains shielding, why lightning rods work, and why a charged metal ball keeps all its charge on its outside.

    The intuition

    A conductor has charges that move freely. Place it in an electric field and the free charges slide: positives one way, negatives the other. They keep moving until the field they create exactly cancels the applied field inside the metal. After that, nothing moves: electrostatic equilibrium. The result: E = 0 everywhere inside a conductor.

    Because E = 0 inside, any extra charge must sit on the surface (Gauss's law: a surface inside the metal encloses no net charge). Just outside, the field is perpendicular to the surface with size σ/ε₀. And since E = 0 inside, V cannot change: the whole conductor is at one potential.

    This means a hollow metal box shields its inside from outside fields (a Faraday cage), and a charge placed inside a cavity attracts an equal and opposite charge onto the cavity wall, leaving the opposite charge on the outer surface.

    • Thinking E = 0 inside a conductor means E = 0 in the cavity. A charge in the cavity does make a field there.
    • Forgetting that a neutral shell with +q in the cavity has −q on the inner wall and +q on the outer wall.
    • Assuming the surface charge is spread evenly on a non-spherical conductor. It gathers at sharp points.

    Properties of a conductor in equilibrium

    • E = 0 everywhere inside the material.
    • Excess charge sits on the outer surface. If there is a cavity with no charge in it, the cavity wall carries no charge either.
    • Field at the surface is perpendicular to it with size σ/ε0.
    • One potential: the whole conductor, including its surface, is an equipotential. Equipotentials hug the surface, and field lines meet it at right angles.
    • Sharp points have a high surface charge density and a strong local field, which is why lightning rods are pointed and why sharp points make sparks.

    Shielding and induction

    • Faraday cage: the field inside an empty closed conducting shell is zero, whatever is outside. (Your car in a thunderstorm.)
    • Charge in a cavity: the cavity wall gets −q, the outer surface gets +q (if the shell was neutral and isolated). The outside field depends only on the total charge and the outer shape, not on where the charge is inside.
    • Charging by induction: bring a charged rod near an isolated conductor, ground it briefly, then remove the ground and the rod. The conductor is left charged, with the sign opposite to the rod.

    Joining conductors

    • Connect two conductors with a wire and charge flows until they are at the same potential, not until they hold the same charge.
    • For two spheres far apart, kQ1/r1 = kQ2/r2, so charge splits in proportion to radius: Q1/Q2 = r1/r2. The smaller sphere then has the larger surface charge density, σ ∝ 1/r, and the stronger field at its surface.
    • Two identical spheres that touch share their total charge equally.
    • Total charge is conserved: add the charges first, then split them by the equal-potential condition.

    Capacitors

    A capacitor stores charge, and with it energy, by holding two conductors close together at different potentials. It is the simplest example of "energy stored in an electric field", and it is the other half of every RC and LC circuit.

    The intuition

    Two metal plates facing each other, with a gap. Connect a battery and charge flows: +Q onto one plate, −Q onto the other, until the plates' voltage matches the battery. The charge you get per volt is the capacitance, C = Q/ΔV. Bigger plates hold more charge for the same voltage (A in the numerator); plates closer together hold more (d in the denominator): C = ε₀A/d.

    The energy of a charged capacitor is U = ½CΔV² = Q²/(2C). It is stored in the field between the plates: the energy per volume is u = ½ε₀E², and it is the same for any electric field anywhere.

    There are two different kinds of "change something on a capacitor" questions, and mixing them up is the most common error. Battery connected: ΔV stays fixed, everything else adjusts. Battery disconnected: Q stays fixed, everything else adjusts. Ask yourself first which one it is.

    Dielectrics

    Slide an insulator between the plates of a capacitor and its capacitance goes up by a factor κ. The insulator's molecules line up against the field and weaken it. The same effect makes insulators attract charged objects, and it is why real capacitors are stuffed with plastic or ceramic.

    The intuition

    An insulator has no free charges, but its molecules can be stretched or turned: the positive end pulled one way by the field, the negative end the other. This is polarization. Inside the slab the little dipoles line up so that the positive ends point toward the negative plate. Stack them up and you get a layer of negative bound charge on the face next to the positive plate, and positive bound charge next to the negative plate.

    These bound charges make their own field, pointing against the field from the plates. The field inside the dielectric drops to E₀/κ, where κ ≥ 1 is the dielectric constant. For the same charge on the plates, a smaller field means a smaller voltage (ΔV = Ed), so C = Q/ΔV grows by a factor κ.

    To keep the story straight, always decide first whether the battery is connected. Isolated (Q fixed): ΔV drops by κ, the field drops by κ, the energy drops by κ. Battery connected (ΔV fixed): Q grows by κ, the field in the gap stays at V/d, the energy grows by κ.

    Common mistakes

    • Using κ as the factor for the field in the wrong direction: E goes down by κ for the same free charge.
    • Not saying whether the battery is connected. Q fixed and V fixed give opposite answers.
    • Treating a dielectric like a conductor. Inside a conductor E = 0; inside a dielectric E = E₀/κ.
    • Forgetting that the energy changes. Something does work as the slab moves.

    What a dielectric does

    • Polarization: the field distorts (or rotates) the molecules into small dipoles aligned with the field.
    • Bound charge: the dipoles leave a surface layer of bound charge on each face. Its surface density is σb = σf(1 − 1/κ), where σf is the free charge on the plates.
    • Net field: E = (σf − σb)/ε0 = σf/(κε0) = E0/κ.
    • The permittivity of the material is ε = κε0. All the vacuum formulas work with ε0 replaced by κε0 inside the dielectric.

    Why real capacitors use them

    • Higher capacitance for the same size (κ ≈ 2–5 plastics, hundreds to thousands for ceramics).
    • Higher breakdown voltage than air (about 3 × 10⁶ V/m for air), so more charge fits before sparks.
    • They keep the plates apart so they can be very close.

    Current & resistance

    Current is the flow of charge, resistance is how hard it is to push, and Ohm's law ties them to voltage. Underneath, it is a crowd of electrons slowly drifting through a lattice of atoms that keeps bumping them: the bumps are the resistance, and the heat they make is the power.

    The intuition

    Electric current is how much charge passes a point each second: I = dQ/dt, in amperes (1 A = 1 C/s). By convention we draw current in the direction positive charges would move, even though in metals it is really electrons moving the other way.

    Switch on a lamp and it lights instantly, yet the electrons in the wire barely creep: a typical drift speed is a fraction of a millimeter per second. The field that pushes them travels near the speed of light along the whole wire at once, like pushing a tube full of marbles: every marble moves the instant you push one end.

    Electrons don't accelerate forever. They keep colliding with the atoms of the metal, losing speed each time, so they reach a steady drift. A longer wire means more collisions to push through (R ∝ L); a wider wire means more paths in parallel (R ∝ 1/A). The energy lost in collisions heats the wire: P = I²R.

    Kirchhoff's rules

    Two rules solve every DC circuit: charge doesn't pile up at a junction, and the voltage changes around any closed loop add to zero. Everything else (series, parallel, voltage dividers) is a shortcut built from them.

    The intuition

    Think of a circuit as a hiking trail map. A battery lifts you up a hill (raises the potential). Each resistor is a downhill slope that uses up some of that height. Go all the way around any closed loop and you must end up at the same height you started: the sum of the voltage changes around a loop is zero.

    Charge is conserved, so at any junction the current flowing in equals the current flowing out. Water in a pipe: no matter how the pipe branches, what goes in comes out.

    In a series circuit there is a single path, so the same current flows through every resistor, and the voltages add. In parallel there are separate paths between the same two points, so each branch has the same voltage, and the currents add. For anything fancier (two batteries, a bridge), write the junction and loop equations and solve.

    The two rules

    • Junction rule (conservation of charge): at any node, the currents entering add to the currents leaving.
    • Loop rule (conservation of energy): going around any closed loop, the potential changes add to zero.
    • Sign conventions: crossing a battery from − to + is +ε; crossing a resistor in the direction of the current is −IR; against the current it is +IR.
    • Strategy: pick directions for every branch current; write one junction equation per independent node and one loop equation per independent loop until you have as many equations as unknowns.

    • Meters: an ideal ammeter has zero resistance and goes in series; an ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance and goes in parallel.

    RC circuits

    Put a resistor in front of a capacitor and it can no longer fill instantly. It fills fast at first and then slows as the capacitor pushes back, following the exponential curve you have already met, with time constant τ = RC.

    The intuition

    An empty capacitor looks like a plain wire at the moment the switch closes: all the battery voltage appears across the resistor, so the current starts at its maximum, ε/R. As charge builds up, the capacitor's own voltage opposes the battery, the voltage across the resistor shrinks, and the current dies away. When the capacitor reaches ε, the current is zero: a full capacitor acts like a broken wire.

    Discharging is the mirror image. Connect the charged capacitor to just the resistor, and it drives a current that starts large and decays as the voltage falls. The charge, voltage and current all decay as e−t/RC.

    A good mental habit: t = 0: capacitor = wire. t → ∞: capacitor = open switch. Then everything in between follows the exponential, with τ = RC controlling how fast.

    3. Starting current. At t = 0 the capacitor acts like a 2000 V battery across the chest: I0 = V0/R = 2000/50 = 40 A.
    4. Time to fall to 10%. Discharging, V = V0e−t/τ. Set e−t/τ = 0.10: t = τ ln 10 = (5.0 ms)(2.30) = 11.5 ms.
    5. Energy delivered by then. Energy goes as V², so at 10% of the voltage only (0.10)² = 1% of the energy is left: 2 J. The other 198 J has gone into the chest.
    6. Check. Tens of amps for a few milliseconds is the right size for a real shock. (Real defibrillators shape the pulse, often reversing it partway through. This is the simple RC model.)

    Common mistakes

    • Using t = RC to mean "fully charged". After one time constant it is only 63% charged.
    • Believing the initial current is zero: for an uncharged capacitor it is the maximum, ε/R.
    • Forgetting that the resistor value does not affect the final charge or the total heat, only the time.
    • Mixing up charge and discharge formulas (1 − e^(−t/τ) vs e^(−t/τ)).

    Charging

    • Loop rule: ε − IR − q/C = 0 with I = dq/dt. Rearranged: dq/dt = (ε − q/C)/R.
    • This is the same equation as terminal speed with drag, so the solution is q = Cε(1 − e−t/RC).
    • The voltage across the resistor is VR = εe−t/RC; the two voltages always add to ε.

    Discharging

    • Loop rule: q/C + R dq/dt = 0 (current flows out of the capacitor), so q = Q0e−t/RC.
    • Energy that was stored all ends up as heat in R: ∫I²R dt = Q0²/(2C).

    Energy bookkeeping while charging

    • Battery supplies Cε²; the capacitor stores ½Cε²; the resistor dissipates the other ½Cε².

    When there is more than one resistor

    • Ask what resistance the capacitor "sees": replace the capacitor with a test source and combine the resistors that are connected across it. That equivalent Req sets τ = ReqC.
    • Use the two limits to find the end points: at t = 0 an uncharged capacitor is a wire (or keeps whatever voltage it had); at t → ∞ the capacitor is an open switch and no current flows through its branch.
    • General recipe: V(t) = V∞ + (V0 − V∞)e−t/τ, where V₀ is the starting voltage and V∞ the final one.

    Magnetic force on charges

    A magnetic field pushes moving charges sideways. The push is always perpendicular to the motion, so it can bend a path into a circle or a spiral but can never speed a charge up or slow it down. That one fact drives mass spectrometers, cyclotrons and auroras.

    The intuition

    A charge sitting still in a magnetic field feels nothing. Make it move and a force appears, at right angles to both the velocity and the field: F = q v × B. Faster charges, stronger fields and bigger charges all feel more force, but only the part of the velocity perpendicular to B counts: F = qvB sin θ.

    Because the force is perpendicular to the velocity, it does no work and cannot change the speed. All it can do is turn the charge. That turns the path into a circle (when the velocity is perpendicular to B), with the magnetic force acting as the centripetal force: qvB = mv²/r, so r = mv/(qB). Lighter, slower particles in stronger fields make tighter circles.

    If the velocity has a component along the field, that component is unaffected and the charge drifts along B while it circles: a helix, or a spiral. The right-hand rule gives the direction: fingers along v, curl them toward B, the thumb points along the force on a positive charge (reverse it for electrons).

    • Zero when v ∥ B (sin 0 = 0): a charge moving along the field feels no force.

    Forces on wires & loops

    A current is moving charge, so a magnetic field pushes a current-carrying wire sideways. Shape the wire into a loop and the pushes on opposite sides make a torque. That torque is the electric motor.

    The intuition

    A wire carrying a current in a magnetic field is just a stream of moving charges, each feeling F = qv × B. Add them up over the length of wire and you get F = I L × B: the force is perpendicular to both the wire and the field, with size ILB sin θ. Again, a wire running along the field feels nothing.

    Bend the wire into a rectangular loop in a uniform field. The two sides that are perpendicular to B get equal and opposite forces, one on each side of the loop. They don't make a net force, but they do make a torque that rotates the loop until its plane is perpendicular to B (its normal lined up with B). The torque is τ = NIAB sin φ, where φ is the angle between the loop's normal and the field.

    An electric motor keeps this going: just as the loop reaches the position where the torque would reverse, a commutator flips the current, so the torque keeps pushing the same way and the loop spins round. Try the spin button with and without the commutator.

    Common mistakes

    • Using the total length of wire in the loop in F = ILB. Only the sides that are perpendicular to B feel a force.
    • Mixing up the angle: τ uses the angle between the normal and B; F uses the angle between the wire and B.
    • Forgetting N: a coil with N turns has N times the force and torque of one loop.
    • Thinking a magnetic field exerts a net force on a closed loop in a uniform field. The net force is zero; only the torque is nonzero.

    • Each charge carrier feels qvd × B; summing over the nAL carriers in a length L gives F = IL × B (here L is a vector along the current).
    • Curved wire: integrate dF = I dℓ × B. In a uniform field the force on a curved wire equals that of a straight wire joining its ends.
    • Two parallel wires attract if their currents are in the same direction and repel if opposite (see Ampère's law): this is how the ampere used to be defined.

    Loops, motors and meters

    • Torque: τ = μ × B with μ = NIA pointing along the right-hand normal (curl your fingers along the current, thumb gives μ).
    • Energy: U = −μ · B; lowest when μ is along B.
    • DC motor: a commutator reverses the current each half turn. Galvanometer (analog meter): the loop turns against a spring until the spring torque balances the magnetic torque, so the deflection is proportional to the current.

    Biot–Savart law

    Coulomb's law builds electric fields from point charges. The Biot–Savart law does the same for magnetism: cut any current into tiny pieces, find the small field each one makes, and add them up. It is the tool when a wire has an awkward shape.

    The intuition

    Each short piece of wire with current I and length dl makes a tiny magnetic field that circles around it, with strength proportional to I dl and falling as 1/r². Crucially, the direction is not along r̂ (as with a point charge) but perpendicular to both the current and the line to the point: dB ∝ I dl × r̂/r². Use the right-hand rule: thumb along the current, fingers curl the way B goes.

    To find the total field of a loop or wire, you add up the dB from all the pieces. Like the charged ring, the slices of a current loop sit at the same distance from a point on the axis, but their fields point in different directions; the sideways parts cancel in pairs and only the axial component survives.

    The result for a straight wire is B = μ₀I/(2πa) for a long wire and a bit smaller for a short one. For a circular loop, B = μ₀I/(2R) at the center. The Biot–Savart law always works, while Ampère's law (next module) is a shortcut that only works with symmetry.

    Using the law

    • Direction: the right-hand rule for dℓ × r̂. For a long wire, the field circles the wire (thumb along I, fingers curl the way B goes).
    • Straight wire segment at perpendicular distance a, spanning angles θ₁ and θ₂ from the perpendicular: B = (μ0I/(4πa))(sin θ1 + sin θ2). For an infinitely long wire, both angles → 90° and B = μ0I/(2πa).
    • Circular arc (angle θ) at its center: B = μ0Iθ/(4πR). A full loop (θ = 2π) gives μ₀I/(2R).
    • Loop on its axis, far away: B ≈ μ0μ/(2πz³) with μ = I·(area): the loop acts like a tiny bar magnet (a magnetic dipole).

    Two parallel wires

    • Wire 1 makes a field μ0I1/(2πd) at wire 2, which then feels a force per length F/L = μ0I1I2/(2πd). Parallel currents attract; opposite currents repel.

    Field of a single moving charge

    • A current is just moving charge, so one charge q moving at velocity v makes B = (μ0/4π) qv × r̂/r² (for speeds much less than c).
    • It is largest in the plane perpendicular to v, and zero straight ahead of or behind the charge.
    • Compare with the electric field of the same charge: B = (v/c²)(E sin θ), a tiny fraction of E at everyday speeds.

    Ampère's law

    Gauss's law finds E when there is symmetry. Ampère's law does the same for B: choose a loop, add up B along it, and the answer depends only on the current passing through the loop. Wires, solenoids and toroids each take one line.

    The intuition

    Walk around a closed loop and add up the part of B that points along your path, times each little step. Ampère's law says this total equals μ₀ times the current that pierces the loop. It is not saying B depends only on that current, but that the loop sum does. When symmetry makes B constant and along the path, the sum is just B times the length of the loop, and you can solve for B immediately.

    For a long straight wire the field circles the wire, so a circular loop is the natural choice: B(2πr) = μ₀I. For a thick wire with current spread evenly, a loop inside the wire encloses only the fraction of the current within it: B grows linearly from zero at the center and then falls off as 1/r outside.

    A long solenoid packs the wire into a coil. A rectangular loop with one side inside and one outside shows that the field inside is uniform, B = μ₀nI, and nearly zero outside. A toroid (a solenoid bent into a doughnut) has B = μ₀NI/(2πr) inside the doughnut and zero outside.

    Faraday & Lenz

    Change the magnetic flux through a loop and an EMF appears in it. This one law drives generators, transformers, microphones and induction stoves, and Lenz's law tells you which way the current goes: always so as to fight the change.

    The intuition

    Hold a loop of wire in a magnetic field: nothing happens. Move a magnet toward it, spin it, or change the field strength: the meter jumps. What matters is not the field itself but how the flux Φ = B A cos θ changes. Faraday's law says the induced EMF equals minus the rate of change of flux, ε = −dΦ/dt (times N for a coil of N turns).

    Three ways to change the flux: change B, change the area A, or change the angle θ. A bigger rate of change, or more turns, gives a bigger EMF. A steady flux, however large, gives no EMF at all.

    The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced current flows in the direction that creates a magnetic field opposing the change in flux. Magnet's north pole pushing in? The coil makes a north face to push back. Nature resists: this is energy conservation, because you must do work to push the magnet in, and that work becomes electrical energy.

    Lenz's law: which way does the induced current flow?

    First decide which way the flux points and whether it is growing or fading. The induced current always fights the change. Taking the loop as seen on the page: a counter-clockwise current makes a field out of the page inside the loop, a clockwise current makes one into the page.

    1. The magnetic field through the loop points into the page and is growing stronger. Which way does the induced current flow?

    Counter-clockwise. The into-page flux is growing, so the loop opposes it by making its own field out of the page inside the loop. By the right-hand rule that needs a counter-clockwise current.

    2. The field through the loop points into the page and is getting weaker. Which way is the induced current?

    Clockwise. The into-page flux is fading, so the loop tries to keep it up: it makes more field into the page, which takes a clockwise current.

    3. The field points out of the page and is growing. Which way is the induced current?

    Clockwise. The out-of-page flux is growing, so the loop opposes it with field into the page: a clockwise current.

    4. The field points out of the page and is fading. Which way is the induced current?

    Counter-clockwise. The loop keeps the fading out-of-page flux going by adding field out of the page: a counter-clockwise current.

    5. You push a bar magnet's north pole toward the loop, so its field points out of the page and the flux grows. Which way is the induced current, and does the loop pull or push the magnet?

    Clockwise. The out-of-page flux grows, so the loop opposes it with field into the page (clockwise). That makes the loop's near face a north pole, which pushes back against the incoming magnet. You do work against this push.

    6. Now you pull the same north pole away, so the out-of-page flux fades. Which way is the induced current, and does the loop pull or push the magnet?

    Counter-clockwise. The flux fades, so the loop keeps it up with field out of the page (counter-clockwise). The near face is now a south pole, which pulls the departing north pole back. Either way the loop fights the change.

    7. The field is steady and points into the page, but the loop is being squeezed smaller. Which way is the induced current?

    Clockwise. A smaller area means fewer into-page field lines through the loop, so the into-page flux drops. The loop opposes the loss by adding field into the page: a clockwise current.

    8. The field is steady and into the page, and the loop is being stretched larger. Which way is the induced current?

    Counter-clockwise. A bigger area lets more into-page flux through, so the loop opposes the gain with field out of the page: a counter-clockwise current.

    9. A long wire runs above the loop, carrying current to the right that is increasing. (Below the wire its field points into the page.) Which way is the loop's induced current?

    Counter-clockwise. A rising current in the wire makes a stronger into-page field at the loop, so the into-page flux grows. The loop opposes it the same way as case 1: counter-clockwise.

    10. On an induction cooktop, the coil below sends a field up through the pan that is rapidly increasing. Which way do the eddy currents circle in the pan, seen from above?

    Clockwise. The upward (out-of-the-pan) flux is rising, so the eddy currents oppose it by making field the other way: clockwise seen from above. Their resistance turns electrical energy into heat, which cooks the food. The pan itself is the circuit.

    11. In a guitar pickup, the steel string (magnetized by the pickup) vibrates toward the coil. As it nears, the upward flux through the coil grows. Seen from above, which way is the induced current while the string approaches?

    Clockwise. The growing upward flux is opposed by a clockwise current (seen from above). As the string vibrates away again the flux falls and the current reverses, so each vibration makes an alternating current: that is the signal sent to the amplifier.

    12. The loop sits in a strong but steady field pointing into the page. Nothing moves or changes. Which way is the induced current?

    No current. Induction responds only to a change in flux. A steady field, however strong, makes a constant flux, so dΦ/dt = 0 and there is no induced EMF or current.

    Common mistakes

    • Writing ε = −dB/dt. It is the flux that matters: B, A and the angle can all change.
    • Using the sign of ε to decide direction without checking with Lenz's law. Always ask: what is changing, and how can the coil oppose it?
    • Thinking a large steady field makes a large EMF. It only matters how fast the flux changes.

    Faraday's law in practice

    • Flux: Φ = ∫ B · dA. For a flat loop in a uniform field, Φ = BA cos θ, where θ is the angle between B and the loop's normal.
    • EMF: ε = −N dΦ/dt. For a graph of Φ against time, the EMF is minus the slope times N.
    • AC generator: a coil rotating at angular speed ω in a field has Φ = BA cos ωt, so ε = NBAω sin ωt: a sine wave.
    • Induced electric field: a changing flux creates a swirling electric field, so ∮ E · dℓ = −dΦB/dt. This field is not conservative: it has no potential.

    Lenz's law

    • Steps: (1) which way is the flux, and is it increasing or decreasing? (2) the induced field opposes the change: if the flux is increasing, it points the other way; if decreasing, the same way; (3) right-hand rule to get the current.
    • Energy: pushing a magnet into a closed loop is harder than pushing it into an open one. The extra work you do becomes heat in the loop.
    • Eddy currents: in a solid conductor (a metal plate) a changing flux makes swirling currents that resist the motion: used for brakes and induction cookers.

    Motional EMF

    A conductor moving through a magnetic field makes a voltage: the field pushes the charges inside it sideways. Complete the circuit and a current flows, which feels a braking force. This is an electric generator in its simplest form, and a clear case where Faraday, Lenz and energy conservation all agree.

    The intuition

    Slide a metal rod to the right through a magnetic field pointing into the page. The charges in the rod move with it, so the magnetic force qv × B pushes the positive charges toward one end and the negative toward the other. That separation creates an EMF between the ends: ε = BLv.

    Put the rod on rails with a resistor and you have a circuit: current I = BLv/R flows. But a current-carrying rod in a magnetic field feels a force F = ILB, and (Lenz's law) it opposes the motion. To keep the rod moving at constant speed you have to push with exactly that force, and the work you do equals the heat produced in the resistor: Fv = I²R.

    If you let go, the rod slows down exponentially with time constant τ = mR/(B²L²): faster means more EMF, more current, more braking. If you keep pushing with a constant force F, the rod speeds up until the braking force equals F: the terminal speed vT = FR/(B²L²), the same form as a falling object with drag.

    Inductors & LR circuits

    A coil of wire resists any change in the current through it, the way a heavy flywheel resists any change in its speed. The inductor is the electromagnetic twin of mass, and an LR circuit follows the same exponential curve as an RC circuit.

    The intuition

    When current flows in a coil it makes a magnetic field through the coil itself. If the current changes, so does the flux through the coil, and Faraday's law makes an EMF that opposes the change: ε = −L dI/dt. The number L is the inductance, in henries (H). A big coil with a lot of turns, or an iron core, has a big L.

    Think of inductance as electrical inertia. A resistor opposes current. An inductor opposes changes in current. Close a switch on an LR circuit and the current cannot jump: at first the inductor takes the whole battery voltage (its back-EMF cancels it), so the current starts at zero. Over time the current climbs toward ε/R and the back-EMF fades.

    The time constant is τ = L/R: a big L (heavy flywheel) makes the current rise slowly; a big R lets it reach its final value quickly. And the energy is stored in the magnetic field: U = ½LI², just as a moving mass stores ½mv².

    Maxwell's equations

    Four equations contain everything about electricity and magnetism. Maxwell noticed one was missing a term, added it, and out popped a wave traveling at 3 × 10⁸ m/s: light is an electromagnetic wave.

    The intuition

    You have already met each of the four laws. Gauss's law says electric field lines start and end on charges. Gauss's law for magnetism says magnetic field lines never start or end: no magnetic monopoles. Faraday's law says a changing magnetic field makes an electric field. Ampère's law says currents make magnetic fields.

    Maxwell realized Ampère's law was incomplete. Charge a capacitor: current flows in the wire, but no charge crosses the gap between the plates, yet the magnetic field circling the wire has to be the same size whether the loop is drawn around the wire or through the gap. His fix: a changing electric field also makes a magnetic field. The extra term, ε₀ dΦE/dt, is the displacement current.

    Now the two curl laws feed each other: a changing B makes E (Faraday), and a changing E makes B (Maxwell). That is a self-sustaining wave, with each field regenerating the other as it travels. Plug in the constants and the speed comes out as c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s: the speed of light. Light, radio waves and X-rays are all the same thing at different frequencies.

    Worked example

    A WiFi router sends out 0.10 W at 2.4 GHz. Treat it as spreading equally in all directions. At your laptop, 3.0 m away, find (a) the wavelength, (b) the intensity, (c) the amplitudes of the electric and magnetic fields, and (d) the direction the wave travels if, at the laptop, E points in +y and B points in +z.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Wavelength. λ = c/f = (3.0 × 10⁸)/(2.4 × 10⁹) = 0.125 m = 12.5 cm. (5 GHz WiFi: 6.0 cm.)
    2. Intensity. The power spreads over a sphere of area 4πr²: I = P/(4πr²) = 0.10/(4π(3.0)²) = 8.8 × 10⁻⁴ W/m².
    3. Electric field amplitude. From I = ½ε0cE0²: E0 = √(2I/(ε0c)) = √(2(8.8 × 10⁻⁴)/((8.85 × 10⁻¹²)(3.0 × 10⁸))) = 0.82 V/m.
    4. Magnetic field amplitude. B0 = E0/c = 0.82/(3.0 × 10⁸) = 2.7 × 10⁻⁹ T, about 20,000 times weaker than Earth's field.
    5. Direction. The wave travels along E × B: ŷ × ẑ = x̂, so +x: away from the router, as it should.
    6. Check. Sunlight is about 1000 W/m², a million times more intense. WiFi works because receivers are extremely sensitive, not because the waves are strong.

    Common mistakes

    • Thinking E and B in a wave are 90° out of phase (like a pendulum). They peak together.
    • Forgetting that the wave moves along E × B, not along E or B.
    • Believing the displacement current is a flow of charge. It is a changing electric field.
    • Using c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s in a medium: in glass or water the wave moves slower.

    Question 1: A sphere with uneven charge

    An insulating solid sphere of radius R has a charge density that grows with distance from its center: ρ(r) = ρ0r/R, where ρ0 is a positive constant. There is no other charge nearby.

    (a) Derive an expression for the total charge Q on the sphere, in terms of ρ0, R and physical constants.

    Scoring guide (2 points)

    Model answer. Q = ∫0R (ρ0r/R)·4πr² dr = (4πρ0/R)(R⁴/4) = πρ0R³.

    (b) Use Gauss's law to derive the electric field magnitude at a distance r < R from the center.

    Scoring guide (3 points)

    Model answer. qenc = ∫0r (ρ0r′/R)4πr′² dr′ = πρ0r⁴/R, so E·4πr² = πρ0r⁴/(ε0R) and E = ρ0r²/(4ε0R). The charge density is not uniform, so the shortcut q_enc = Q(r/R)³ is wrong here.

    (c) Derive the field magnitude at a distance r > R, and show that your answers to (b) and (c) agree at r = R.

    Scoring guide (2 points)

    Model answer. E = Q/(4πε0r²) = ρ0R³/(4ε0r²). At r = R both give ρ0R/(4ε0). The field is continuous because there is no surface charge.

    (d) Taking V = 0 infinitely far away, derive the electric potential at the center of the sphere.

    Scoring guide (3 points)

    Model answer. V(R) = kQ/R = ρ0R²/(4ε0). Moving inward against the outward field raises the potential by ∫0R ρ0r²/(4ε0R) dr = ρ0R²/(12ε0). So V(0) = ρ0R²/(4ε0) + ρ0R²/(12ε0) = ρ0R²/(3ε0).

    Question 2: Charging a capacitor

    A battery of EMF ℰ with negligible internal resistance, a resistor R, an uncharged capacitor C and an open switch are connected in series. The switch is closed at t = 0.

    (a) On separate axes, sketch the current I in the resistor and the potential difference VC across the capacitor as functions of time. Label the values at t = 0 and the values approached as t → ∞.

    Scoring guide (3 points)

    Model answer. At first the empty capacitor acts like a plain wire, so all of ℰ is across the resistor and I = ℰ/R. As charge builds up, VC grows, leaving less voltage for the resistor, so the current drops. Open the guide to see the graphs.

    (b) Apply Kirchhoff's loop rule to write a differential equation for the charge q(t) on the capacitor. Show that I(t) = (ℰ/R)e−t/RC is consistent with your sketch.

    Scoring guide (2 points)

    Model answer. ℰ − R dq/dt − q/C = 0. The given current equals ℰ/R at t = 0 and falls by a factor of e every RC seconds, approaching zero: the shape sketched in (a).

    (c) Derive the total energy supplied by the battery, the energy stored in the capacitor, and the energy dissipated in the resistor, all after a long time.

    Scoring guide (3 points)

    Model answer. The final charge is Q = Cℰ. The battery does work Qℰ = Cℰ². The capacitor stores ½Cℰ². The other ½Cℰ² becomes heat in the resistor, whatever the value of R.

    (d) The resistor is replaced by one with resistance 2R and the experiment is repeated. Describe how each of your two sketches from (a) changes, and justify your answer.

    Scoring guide (2 points)

    Model answer. The starting current halves to ℰ/(2R) and the time constant doubles to 2RC, so both curves stretch out in time. The final values (I → 0, V_C → ℰ) do not change, because they don't depend on R.

    Question 4: A loop falling into a magnetic field

    A square wire loop with side L, mass m and resistance R is held in a vertical plane above a region of uniform magnetic field B that points into the page. The field region has a horizontal top edge and extends far below. The loop is released, falls, and its bottom edge enters the field while the top edge is still outside.

    (a) Without writing equations, predict the direction of the induced current in the loop while it is entering the field, and whether the magnetic force on the loop helps or opposes its fall. Explain using physics principles.

    Scoring guide (3 points)

    Model answer. More of the loop enters the field, so the flux into the page grows. By Lenz's law the induced current makes its own field out of the page inside the loop, which takes a counterclockwise current. The force on the bottom edge, IL × B, then points up, opposing the fall.

    (b) Derive an expression for the terminal speed the loop would reach if it were still partly outside the field, in terms of m, g, R, B and L.

    Scoring guide (4 points)

    Model answer. ℰ = BLv, I = BLv/R, F = ILB = B²L²v/R. At terminal speed B²L²vT/R = mg, so vT = mgR/(B²L²).

    (c) Explain how your result in (b) agrees with your reasoning in (a). Then describe the loop's motion after it is completely inside the field, and justify it.

    Scoring guide (3 points)

    Model answer. The opposing force from (a) grows with speed, which is why there is a terminal speed at all. A larger R weakens the current and the force, so the loop must go faster before they balance: v_T ∝ R, as in (b). Once the whole loop is inside the uniform field the flux stays constant, so there is no current, no magnetic force, and it falls freely with acceleration g.

    Which tool do I use?

    Half the battle in E&M is choosing the right law. Learn the clues, then practice picking.

    The intuition

    Each law answers a different kind of question. Coulomb's law is the brute-force way to add up charges. Gauss's law is the shortcut when the charge has symmetry. Potential and energy make problems about speed and work easy because they are scalars. Kirchhoff's rules handle circuits. Magnetic force laws handle moving charges and currents. Biot–Savart and Ampère's law find magnetic fields, and Faraday's law handles anything that changes with time.

    Ask first: is the question about a field, a voltage, a current in a circuit, a force from B, or something that changes?
