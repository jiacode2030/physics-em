Capacitors

A capacitor stores charge, and with it energy, by holding two conductors close together at different potentials. It is the simplest example of "energy stored in an electric field", and it is the other half of every RC and LC circuit.

The intuition

Two metal plates facing each other, with a gap. Connect a battery and charge flows: +Q onto one plate, −Q onto the other, until the plates' voltage matches the battery. The charge you get per volt is the capacitance, C = Q/ΔV. Bigger plates hold more charge for the same voltage (A in the numerator); plates closer together hold more (d in the denominator): C = ε₀A/d.

The energy of a charged capacitor is U = ½CΔV² = Q²/(2C). It is stored in the field between the plates: the energy per volume is u = ½ε₀E², and it is the same for any electric field anywhere.

There are two different kinds of "change something on a capacitor" questions, and mixing them up is the most common error. Battery connected: ΔV stays fixed, everything else adjusts. Battery disconnected: Q stays fixed, everything else adjusts. Ask yourself first which one it is.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. With the battery connected, double the plate separation.

    What you should see

    C halves. The battery keeps ΔV = 12 V, so Q halves: charge flows back to the battery. E = ΔV/d halves too. Energy ½CΔV² halves.

  2. Disconnect the battery, then double the separation.

    What you should see

    Now Q cannot change. C halves, so ΔV = Q/C doubles. The field E = Q/(ε₀A) does not change at all: it depends only on the charge density. Energy Q²/2C doubles, and the extra energy is the work you did pulling the plates apart against their attraction.

  3. Series: 4 μF and 2 μF on 12 V. Then switch to parallel.

    What you should see

    In series C_eq = 1.33 μF (less than either), each gets the same 16 μC, and the smaller capacitor takes the bigger voltage (8 V vs 4 V). In parallel C_eq = 6 μF (the sum), each has 12 V, and the larger capacitor holds more charge.

Worked example

A phone's touchscreen senses your finger as a capacitor. Model it as parallel plates: your fingertip is one plate, a 4.0 mm × 4.0 mm electrode under the glass is the other, and the cover glass between them is 0.50 mm thick with κ = 7.0 (glass multiplies the capacitance by κ: see Dielectrics). The sensor drives the electrode to 3.0 V. (a) Find C, Q, the field in the glass and the stored energy. (b) Now your finger hovers 1.0 mm above the glass. What is the capacitance, and why does the screen ignore it?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Capacitance. A = (4.0 × 10⁻³ m)² = 1.6 × 10⁻⁵ m². Then C = κε0A/d = (7.0)(8.85 × 10⁻¹²)(1.6 × 10⁻⁵)/(0.50 × 10⁻³) = 1.98 × 10⁻¹² F ≈ 2.0 pF.
  2. Charge. Q = CΔV = (1.98 × 10⁻¹² F)(3.0 V) = 5.9 × 10⁻¹² C (5.9 pC, about 37 million electrons).
  3. Field in the glass. E = ΔV/d = 3.0/(0.50 × 10⁻³) = 6.0 × 10³ V/m, pointing from the electrode to the finger.
  4. Energy. U = ½CΔV² = ½(1.98 × 10⁻¹²)(3.0)² = 8.9 × 10⁻¹² J. Tiny, but the chip only has to measure C.
  5. Hovering finger. Now there is 1.0 mm of air on top of the glass. That is a second capacitor in series: Cair = ε0A/d = (8.85 × 10⁻¹²)(1.6 × 10⁻⁵)/(1.0 × 10⁻³) = 0.142 pF, and 1/C = 1/1.98 + 1/0.142, so C = 0.13 pF. In series the small capacitor wins.
  6. Check. Touching changes C by a factor of 15 (= 1 + κ × 1.0 mm/0.50 mm), so the chip can easily tell a touch from a hover. Real screens use a grid of electrodes and sense small changes, but the idea is the same.

Common mistakes

  • Using ΔV = constant after the battery has been disconnected (or Q constant with the battery on).
  • Adding capacitances in series. They add in parallel; in series it is the reciprocals.
  • Using the battery voltage for each capacitor in a series pair. The battery voltage is shared.
  • Forgetting to convert mm and cm² to SI units in C = ε₀A/d.

Parallel plates

  • Field: between large plates the field is uniform, E = σ/ε0 = Q/(ε0A) (the fields of the two sheets add between them and cancel outside).
  • Voltage: ΔV = Ed = Qd/(ε0A), so C = Q/ΔV = ε0A/d. It depends only on the geometry.
  • Other shapes: a spherical capacitor has C = 4πε0ab/(b − a); a cylindrical one has C = 2πε0L/ln(b/a). Find each by Gauss's law, then integrate E to get ΔV.

Energy

  • U = ∫ ΔV dq = ∫ (q/C) dq = Q²/(2C): each extra bit of charge costs more energy, because the voltage is higher.
  • Energy density: u = U/(Ad) = ½ε0E².

Combining capacitors

  • Parallel: same ΔV, charges add: Ceq = ΣCi.
  • Series: same Q, voltages add: 1/Ceq = Σ 1/Ci.
