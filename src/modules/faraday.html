Faraday & Lenz

Change the magnetic flux through a loop and an EMF appears in it. This one law drives generators, transformers, microphones and induction stoves, and Lenz's law tells you which way the current goes: always so as to fight the change.

The intuition

Hold a loop of wire in a magnetic field: nothing happens. Move a magnet toward it, spin it, or change the field strength: the meter jumps. What matters is not the field itself but how the flux Φ = B A cos θ changes. Faraday's law says the induced EMF equals minus the rate of change of flux, ε = −dΦ/dt (times N for a coil of N turns).

Three ways to change the flux: change B, change the area A, or change the angle θ. A bigger rate of change, or more turns, gives a bigger EMF. A steady flux, however large, gives no EMF at all.

The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced current flows in the direction that creates a magnetic field opposing the change in flux. Magnet's north pole pushing in? The coil makes a north face to push back. Nature resists: this is energy conservation, because you must do work to push the magnet in, and that work becomes electrical energy.

Lenz's law: which way does the induced current flow?

First decide which way the flux points and whether it is growing or fading. The induced current always fights the change. Taking the loop as seen on the page: a counter-clockwise current makes a field out of the page inside the loop, a clockwise current makes one into the page.

1. The magnetic field through the loop points into the page and is growing stronger. Which way does the induced current flow?

Counter-clockwise. The into-page flux is growing, so the loop opposes it by making its own field out of the page inside the loop. By the right-hand rule that needs a counter-clockwise current.

2. The field through the loop points into the page and is getting weaker. Which way is the induced current?

Clockwise. The into-page flux is fading, so the loop tries to keep it up: it makes more field into the page, which takes a clockwise current.

3. The field points out of the page and is growing. Which way is the induced current?

Clockwise. The out-of-page flux is growing, so the loop opposes it with field into the page: a clockwise current.

4. The field points out of the page and is fading. Which way is the induced current?

Counter-clockwise. The loop keeps the fading out-of-page flux going by adding field out of the page: a counter-clockwise current.

5. You push a bar magnet's north pole toward the loop, so its field points out of the page and the flux grows. Which way is the induced current, and does the loop pull or push the magnet?

Clockwise. The out-of-page flux grows, so the loop opposes it with field into the page (clockwise). That makes the loop's near face a north pole, which pushes back against the incoming magnet. You do work against this push.

6. Now you pull the same north pole away, so the out-of-page flux fades. Which way is the induced current, and does the loop pull or push the magnet?

Counter-clockwise. The flux fades, so the loop keeps it up with field out of the page (counter-clockwise). The near face is now a south pole, which pulls the departing north pole back. Either way the loop fights the change.

7. The field is steady and points into the page, but the loop is being squeezed smaller. Which way is the induced current?

Clockwise. A smaller area means fewer into-page field lines through the loop, so the into-page flux drops. The loop opposes the loss by adding field into the page: a clockwise current.

8. The field is steady and into the page, and the loop is being stretched larger. Which way is the induced current?

Counter-clockwise. A bigger area lets more into-page flux through, so the loop opposes the gain with field out of the page: a counter-clockwise current.

9. A long wire runs above the loop, carrying current to the right that is increasing. (Below the wire its field points into the page.) Which way is the loop's induced current?

Counter-clockwise. A rising current in the wire makes a stronger into-page field at the loop, so the into-page flux grows. The loop opposes it the same way as case 1: counter-clockwise.

10. On an induction cooktop, the coil below sends a field up through the pan that is rapidly increasing. Which way do the eddy currents circle in the pan, seen from above?

Clockwise. The upward (out-of-the-pan) flux is rising, so the eddy currents oppose it by making field the other way: clockwise seen from above. Their resistance turns electrical energy into heat, which cooks the food. The pan itself is the circuit.

11. In a guitar pickup, the steel string (magnetized by the pickup) vibrates toward the coil. As it nears, the upward flux through the coil grows. Seen from above, which way is the induced current while the string approaches?

Clockwise. The growing upward flux is opposed by a clockwise current (seen from above). As the string vibrates away again the flux falls and the current reverses, so each vibration makes an alternating current: that is the signal sent to the amplifier.

12. The loop sits in a strong but steady field pointing into the page. Nothing moves or changes. Which way is the induced current?

No current. Induction responds only to a change in flux. A steady field, however strong, makes a constant flux, so dΦ/dt = 0 and there is no induced EMF or current.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Let the magnet swing automatically. Watch the EMF as it passes through the coil.

    What you should see

    The EMF is largest when the magnet is moving fastest through the strongly changing part of the field, and it is zero at the instant the flux is at its maximum (centered) and at the ends of the swing.

  2. Double the number of turns.

    What you should see

    Each turn gets the same EMF, and they add in series, so ε doubles. The flux per turn is unchanged.

  3. Double the swing frequency.

    What you should see

    The same flux change happens in half the time, so the EMF doubles (and the graph squeezes horizontally).

  4. Switch to dragging. Move the magnet in slowly, then quickly.

    What you should see

    Slow motion gives a small EMF; fast motion gives a large one. Hold the magnet still inside the coil: the EMF is exactly zero, even though the flux is at its maximum.

Worked example

A wireless phone charger drives an alternating current in its coil at f = 125 kHz. In the phone, a flat receiver coil of 12 turns and radius 2.0 cm lies right over it, and the field through the coil (perpendicular to it) swings between +0.50 mT and −0.50 mT. (a) Find the change in flux per turn during one half-cycle. (b) Find the average EMF during that half-cycle. (c) Find the peak EMF. (d) At an instant when the field points up through the coil and is growing, which way does the induced current flow, seen from above?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Area. A = π(0.020 m)² = 1.26 × 10⁻³ m². The field is perpendicular to the coil, so cos θ = 1.
  2. Flux change in a half-cycle. The field goes from +0.50 mT to −0.50 mT, a change of 1.0 mT, not 0.50: ΔΦ = (−0.50 − 0.50) × 10⁻³ T × 1.26 × 10⁻³ m² = −1.26 × 10⁻⁶ Wb per turn.
  3. Average EMF. A half-cycle lasts Δt = 1/(2f) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ s. So |ε| = N|ΔΦ|/Δt = (12)(1.26 × 10⁻⁶)/(4.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 3.8 V.
  4. Peak EMF. The flux is Φ = B0A cos ωt, so ε = NB0Aω sin ωt with ω = 2πf = 7.85 × 10⁵ rad/s: εpeak = (12)(0.50 × 10⁻³)(1.26 × 10⁻³)(7.85 × 10⁵) = 5.9 V.
  5. Direction (Lenz). The flux up through the coil is growing. The coil opposes that by making its own field down inside it, which takes a clockwise current seen from above (right-hand rule: thumb down, fingers curl clockwise as you look down).
  6. Check. The peak is π/2 times the average, as it should be for a sine wave ✓. The field is only about 10 times Earth's, but it changes 125,000 times a second, and the EMF depends on how fast the flux changes.

Common mistakes

  • Writing ε = −dB/dt. It is the flux that matters: B, A and the angle can all change.
  • Using the sign of ε to decide direction without checking with Lenz's law. Always ask: what is changing, and how can the coil oppose it?
  • Thinking a large steady field makes a large EMF. It only matters how fast the flux changes.
  • Forgetting the factor of N for a coil with many turns.

Faraday's law in practice

  • Flux: Φ = ∫ B · dA. For a flat loop in a uniform field, Φ = BA cos θ, where θ is the angle between B and the loop's normal.
  • EMF: ε = −N dΦ/dt. For a graph of Φ against time, the EMF is minus the slope times N.
  • AC generator: a coil rotating at angular speed ω in a field has Φ = BA cos ωt, so ε = NBAω sin ωt: a sine wave.
  • Induced electric field: a changing flux creates a swirling electric field, so ∮ E · dℓ = −dΦB/dt. This field is not conservative: it has no potential.

Lenz's law

  • Steps: (1) which way is the flux, and is it increasing or decreasing? (2) the induced field opposes the change: if the flux is increasing, it points the other way; if decreasing, the same way; (3) right-hand rule to get the current.
  • Energy: pushing a magnet into a closed loop is harder than pushing it into an open one. The extra work you do becomes heat in the loop.
  • Eddy currents: in a solid conductor (a metal plate) a changing flux makes swirling currents that resist the motion: used for brakes and induction cookers.
