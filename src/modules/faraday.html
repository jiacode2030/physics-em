Faraday & Lenz

Change the magnetic flux through a loop and an EMF appears in it. This one law drives generators, transformers, microphones and induction stoves, and Lenz's law tells you which way the current goes: always so as to fight the change.

The intuition

Hold a loop of wire in a magnetic field: nothing happens. Move a magnet toward it, spin it, or change the field strength: the meter jumps. What matters is not the field itself but how the flux Φ = B A cos θ changes. Faraday's law says the induced EMF equals minus the rate of change of flux, ε = −dΦ/dt (times N for a coil of N turns).

Three ways to change the flux: change B, change the area A, or change the angle θ. A bigger rate of change, or more turns, gives a bigger EMF. A steady flux, however large, gives no EMF at all.

The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced current flows in the direction that creates a magnetic field opposing the change in flux. Magnet's north pole pushing in? The coil makes a north face to push back. Nature resists: this is energy conservation, because you must do work to push the magnet in, and that work becomes electrical energy.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Let the magnet swing automatically. Watch the EMF as it passes through the coil.

    What you should see

    The EMF is largest when the magnet is moving fastest through the strongly changing part of the field, and it is zero at the instant the flux is at its maximum (centered) and at the ends of the swing.

  2. Double the number of turns.

    What you should see

    Each turn gets the same EMF, and they add in series, so ε doubles. The flux per turn is unchanged.

  3. Double the swing frequency.

    What you should see

    The same flux change happens in half the time, so the EMF doubles (and the graph squeezes horizontally).

  4. Switch to dragging. Move the magnet in slowly, then quickly.

    What you should see

    Slow motion gives a small EMF; fast motion gives a large one. Hold the magnet still inside the coil: the EMF is exactly zero, even though the flux is at its maximum.

Worked example

A flat coil of 200 turns and radius 4.0 cm sits with its plane perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field. The field decreases from 0.50 T to zero in 0.10 s. (a) Find the average induced EMF. (b) If the coil's resistance is 10 Ω, find the average current. (c) Which way does the current flow if B points into the page?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Flux change. A = π(0.040)² = 5.03 × 10⁻³ m². The field is perpendicular to the plane, so cos θ = 1: ΔΦ = ΔB·A = (0 − 0.50)(5.03 × 10⁻³) = −2.5 × 10⁻³ Wb per turn.
  2. EMF. ε = −NΔΦ/Δt = −(200)(−2.5 × 10⁻³)/0.10 = +5.0 V. (The size is what matters here: 5.0 V.)
  3. Current. I = ε/R = 5.0/10 = 0.50 A.
  4. Direction (Lenz). The flux into the page is decreasing. The coil opposes that by making its own field into the page inside it, which requires a clockwise current (right-hand rule: fingers curl clockwise, thumb into the page).
  5. Check. Larger N, larger A, bigger field change or faster change would each increase ε. Nothing would happen at all if B were constant ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Writing ε = −dB/dt. It is the flux that matters: B, A and the angle can all change.
  • Using the sign of ε to decide direction without checking with Lenz's law. Always ask: what is changing, and how can the coil oppose it?
  • Thinking a large steady field makes a large EMF. It only matters how fast the flux changes.
  • Forgetting the factor of N for a coil with many turns.

Faraday's law in practice

  • Flux: Φ = ∫ B · dA. For a flat loop in a uniform field, Φ = BA cos θ, where θ is the angle between B and the loop's normal.
  • EMF: ε = −N dΦ/dt. For a graph of Φ against time, the EMF is minus the slope times N.
  • AC generator: a coil rotating at angular speed ω in a field has Φ = BA cos ωt, so ε = NBAω sin ωt: a sine wave.
  • Induced electric field: a changing flux creates a swirling electric field, so ∮ E · dℓ = −dΦB/dt. This field is not conservative: it has no potential.

Lenz's law

  • Steps: (1) which way is the flux, and is it increasing or decreasing? (2) the induced field opposes the change: if the flux is increasing, it points the other way; if decreasing, the same way; (3) right-hand rule to get the current.
  • Energy: pushing a magnet into a closed loop is harder than pushing it into an open one. The extra work you do becomes heat in the loop.
  • Eddy currents: in a solid conductor (a metal plate) a changing flux makes swirling currents that resist the motion: used for brakes and induction cookers.
