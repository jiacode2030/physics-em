Current & resistance

Current is the flow of charge, resistance is how hard it is to push, and Ohm's law ties them to voltage. Underneath, it is a crowd of electrons slowly drifting through a lattice of atoms that keeps bumping them: the bumps are the resistance, and the heat they make is the power.

The intuition

Electric current is how much charge passes a point each second: I = dQ/dt, in amperes (1 A = 1 C/s). By convention we draw current in the direction positive charges would move, even though in metals it is really electrons moving the other way.

Switch on a lamp and it lights instantly, yet the electrons in the wire barely creep: a typical drift speed is a fraction of a millimeter per second. The field that pushes them travels near the speed of light along the whole wire at once, like pushing a tube full of marbles: every marble moves the instant you push one end.

Electrons don't accelerate forever. They keep colliding with the atoms of the metal, losing speed each time, so they reach a steady drift. A longer wire means more collisions to push through (R ∝ L); a wider wire means more paths in parallel (R ∝ 1/A). The energy lost in collisions heats the wire: P = I²R.

Electrons are drawn jiggling at random (they really move at about 10⁶ m/s) plus a slow drift against the current. The drift is drawn about 10⁴ times faster than it really is, so that you can see it. Real copper drift speeds are fractions of a millimeter per second.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Double the current. Then double the diameter.

    What you should see

    Double I and the drift speed, voltage drop (IR) and current density double; power (I²R) quadruples. Now double the diameter: the area quadruples, so R drops to a quarter and the drift speed drops to a quarter.

  2. Switch from copper to nichrome (the heater-wire material).

    What you should see

    Nichrome's resistivity is about 65 times copper's. The same current makes 65 times the voltage drop and heat. That is why toaster elements are made of it.

  3. Double the length with a fixed current.

    What you should see

    Resistance doubles, so the voltage needed doubles and the heating doubles. The drift speed and current density are unchanged: they depend only on I and the cross-section.

  4. Make the wire very thin (0.5 mm) at 10 A.

    What you should see

    Resistance is 16 times larger than for 2 mm wire; the heat is huge. This is why thin extension cords overheat.

Worked example

A copper wire of diameter 2.0 mm and length 10 m carries a current of 5.0 A. Copper has resistivity ρ = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m and 8.5 × 10²⁸ free electrons per m³. Find the current density, the drift speed, the resistance, the voltage drop and the power lost.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Cross-sectional area. A = π(1.0 × 10⁻³ m)² = 3.14 × 10⁻⁶ m². Careful: the diameter is 2.0 mm, so the radius is 1.0 mm.
  2. Current density. J = I/A = 5.0/(3.14 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.6 × 10⁶ A/m².
  3. Drift speed. vd = J/(ne) = (1.6 × 10⁶)/((8.5 × 10²⁸)(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)) = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ m/s = 0.12 mm/s. Surprisingly slow.
  4. Resistance. R = ρL/A = (1.68 × 10⁻⁸)(10)/(3.14 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.054 Ω.
  5. Voltage drop and power. ΔV = IR = (5.0)(0.054) = 0.27 V and P = I²R = (5.0)²(0.054) = 1.3 W.
  6. Check. Power should also equal IΔV = 5.0 × 0.27 = 1.3 W ✓. A 10 m extension cord warming a little at 5 A is realistic.

Common mistakes

  • Using the diameter instead of the radius in A = πr².
  • Thinking electrons travel through the wire at the speed of light. The field does; the electrons drift slowly.
  • Using R = V/I as if resistance depended on the voltage. For a given wire R is fixed by ρ, L and A (for an ohmic material).
  • Forgetting the direction: conventional current is opposite to electron flow.

The microscopic picture

  • Drift velocity: in time t the charge crossing a wire's cross-section is the charge in a length vdt of wire: Q = (nAvdt)q. So I = nqvdA.
  • Current density: J = I/A = nqvd (A/m²), and in an ohmic material J = σE = E/ρ: the field in the wire pushes the charges.
  • Ohm's law in its microscopic form is E = ρJ. Multiply by L and A to get ΔV = IR with R = ρL/A.

Resistance and power

  • Resistivity ρ depends on the material and its temperature; for metals it rises as they heat up.
  • Power: P = IΔV is the rate energy leaves the circuit element. Over a resistor that is heat: I²R = ΔV²/R.
  • Non-ohmic parts (diodes, bulb filaments) don't have a constant R: their I–V graph is not a straight line.
