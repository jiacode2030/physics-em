Magnetic force on charges

A magnetic field pushes moving charges sideways. The push is always perpendicular to the motion, so it can bend a path into a circle or a spiral but can never speed a charge up or slow it down. That one fact drives mass spectrometers, cyclotrons and auroras.

The intuition

A charge sitting still in a magnetic field feels nothing. Make it move and a force appears, at right angles to both the velocity and the field: F = q v × B. Faster charges, stronger fields and bigger charges all feel more force, but only the part of the velocity perpendicular to B counts: F = qvB sin θ.

Because the force is perpendicular to the velocity, it does no work and cannot change the speed. All it can do is turn the charge. That turns the path into a circle (when the velocity is perpendicular to B), with the magnetic force acting as the centripetal force: qvB = mv²/r, so r = mv/(qB). Lighter, slower particles in stronger fields make tighter circles.

If the velocity has a component along the field, that component is unaffected and the charge drifts along B while it circles: a helix, or a spiral. The right-hand rule gives the direction: fingers along v, curl them toward B, the thumb points along the force on a positive charge (reverse it for electrons).

Right-hand rule: which way is the force or field?

Look at the setup, use your right hand, then choose. The answer diagram appears when you get it right. For an electron, find the direction for a positive charge and flip it.

1. A positive charge moves to the right. The magnetic field points into the page. Which way is the force on it?

Up. Right hand: fingers point along v (right), curl into B (into the page), thumb points up. F = qv × B.

2. An electron moves to the right. The field points into the page. Which way is the force?

Down. The right-hand rule gives up for a positive charge, so for the negative electron the force flips to down.

3. A positive charge moves straight up. The field points out of the page. Which way is the force?

Right. Fingers up (v), curl out of the page (B), thumb points right.

4. A positive charge moves to the left. The field points into the page. Which way is the force?

Down. Reverse of case 1: flip the velocity and the force flips too.

5. A proton moves into the page (⊗). The field points straight up, in the plane of the page. Which way is the force?

Right. Point the right fingers into the page (v), curl them up (B); the thumb points right. F = qv × B.

6. A long wire carries current to the right. At a point directly above the wire, which way does its magnetic field point?

Out of the page. Grab the wire with the right hand, thumb along the current (right). Above the wire the fingers come out of the page.

7. Same wire, current to the right. At a point directly below it, which way does the field point?

Into the page. Below the wire the curled fingers of the right hand go into the page. The field circles the wire.

8. A wire carries current straight up, sitting in a field that points to the right. Which way is the force on the wire?

Into the page. F = IL × B: fingers up (current), curl toward the right (B), thumb into the page.

9. A wire carries current to the right, in a field that points up. Which way is the force on the wire?

Out of the page. F = IL × B: fingers right (current), curl up (B), thumb out of the page.

10. Two parallel wires both carry current to the right. Which way is the force on the top wire?

Toward the other wire. Parallel currents in the same direction attract. (Each wire sits in the other's field; work the rule through and the forces pull them together.)

11. Now the top wire carries current to the right and the bottom wire to the left. Which way is the force on the top wire?

Away from the other wire. Antiparallel (opposite) currents repel.

12. A positive charge sits at rest in a field that points into the page. What is the magnetic force on it?

No force. The magnetic force is qv × B. With v = 0 there is nothing for the field to push on. A charge must be moving to feel a magnetic force.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Compare an electron and a proton at the same speed and field.

    What you should see

    The electron and the proton circle in opposite directions (opposite charges), and the proton's circle is about 1800 times larger because it is about 1800 times heavier.

  2. Tilt the velocity: set the angle to 30° so there is a component along B.

    What you should see

    The circle shrinks (only v sin θ counts for the radius) and the particle spirals along B. The pitch is v cos θ times the period.

  3. Double the magnetic field.

    What you should see

    The radius halves and the period halves: the particle circles twice as often. The speed has not changed.

  4. In the velocity selector, tune the speed to exactly E/B.

    What you should see

    At 100 km/s = E/B, the electric and magnetic forces cancel and the proton flies straight through. Move the speed either way and it bends.

Worked example

The aurora: an electron from the solar wind arrives high above the Arctic at 2.0 × 10⁷ m/s (about 1 keV), moving at 30° to Earth's magnetic field, which there is about 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ T. Find (a) the magnetic force on it, (b) the radius of the spiral it follows, (c) the time for one turn and (d) how far along the field it moves per turn. Why do these electrons end up near the poles?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Split the velocity. Only the part perpendicular to B feels a force: v⊥ = v sin 30° = 1.0 × 10⁷ m/s. The part along B is v∥ = v cos 30° = 1.73 × 10⁷ m/s.
  2. Force. F = ev⊥B = (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)(1.0 × 10⁷)(5.0 × 10⁻⁵) = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ N. It is perpendicular to both v and B, so it always points toward the axis of the spiral.
  3. Radius. r = mv⊥/(eB) = (9.11 × 10⁻³¹)(1.0 × 10⁷)/((1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁵)) = 1.1 m.
  4. Time for one turn. T = 2πm/(eB) = 2π(9.11 × 10⁻³¹)/((1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁵)) = 7.2 × 10⁻⁷ s. It does not depend on the speed.
  5. Distance along the field per turn. p = v∥T = (1.73 × 10⁷)(7.2 × 10⁻⁷) = 12 m.
  6. Why the poles? The magnetic force never has a part along B, so v∥ never changes: the electron is tied to its field line like a bead on a wire, in a corkscrew about 2 m wide. Earth's field lines dip into the atmosphere near the magnetic poles, so that is where the electrons hit the air (about 100 km up) and make it glow. Check: mv⊥²/r = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ N ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Using v instead of v sin θ for the force or the radius.
  • Getting the direction wrong for a negative charge. The right-hand rule gives the force on a positive charge; for an electron reverse it.
  • Thinking the magnetic force speeds the particle up. It does no work: the speed is constant.
  • Using B in mT in the formula without converting to tesla.

Motion in a magnetic field

  • Perpendicular to B: uniform circular motion with r = mv/(|q|B). Kinetic energy is constant.
  • Cyclotron frequency f = |q|B/(2πm) does not depend on speed or radius, which is what makes cyclotron accelerators work.
  • At an angle: the perpendicular part circles, the parallel part drifts: pitch p = v∥T. Charges spiral along Earth's field lines toward the poles and make the aurora.
  • Zero when v ∥ B (sin 0 = 0): a charge moving along the field feels no force.

Crossed fields and applications

  • Velocity selector: E and B perpendicular, with qE = qvB for the chosen speed v = E/B. Independent of charge and mass.
  • Mass spectrometer: select the speed, then send the ions into a second field. They circle with r = mv/(qB), so m = qBr/v: measure where they land to find the mass.
  • Hall effect: in a current-carrying conductor in a field, charges pile up on one side, creating a measurable transverse voltage; its sign shows whether the carriers are positive or negative.
