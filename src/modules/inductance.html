Inductors & LR circuits

A coil of wire resists any change in the current through it, the way a heavy flywheel resists any change in its speed. The inductor is the electromagnetic twin of mass, and an LR circuit follows the same exponential curve as an RC circuit.

The intuition

When current flows in a coil it makes a magnetic field through the coil itself. If the current changes, so does the flux through the coil, and Faraday's law makes an EMF that opposes the change: ε = −L dI/dt. The number L is the inductance, in henries (H). A big coil with a lot of turns, or an iron core, has a big L.

Think of inductance as electrical inertia. A resistor opposes current. An inductor opposes changes in current. Close a switch on an LR circuit and the current cannot jump: at first the inductor takes the whole battery voltage (its back-EMF cancels it), so the current starts at zero. Over time the current climbs toward ε/R and the back-EMF fades.

The time constant is τ = L/R: a big L (heavy flywheel) makes the current rise slowly; a big R lets it reach its final value quickly. And the energy is stored in the magnetic field: U = ½LI², just as a moving mass stores ½mv².

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Close the switch and watch the two voltages.

    What you should see

    At the start the inductor has all 12 V across it (back-EMF cancels the battery) and the current is zero. As the current rises, the voltage across the resistor grows and the inductor voltage falls. At the end the inductor has no voltage across it: it acts like a plain wire.

  2. Double the inductance and close the switch.

    What you should see

    The time constant doubles: the current rises more slowly. The final current is the same, ε/R, because L doesn't matter once everything is steady.

  3. Close the switch, wait until it levels off, then open it.

    What you should see

    The inductor keeps the current going through the resistor: the current decays exponentially and the inductor's voltage reverses sign. The stored magnetic energy turns into heat in R.

  4. Increase R to 200 Ω.

    What you should see

    The time constant shrinks (L/R = 1 ms) and the final current falls to ε/R = 60 mA. Faster, but weaker.

Worked example

How does a car make a 20,000 V spark from a 12 V battery? The ignition coil's primary winding has L = 6.0 mH and a resistance of 2.0 Ω. A switch connects it to the 12 V battery. Find (a) the time constant, (b) the current it would reach if left on, (c) the current after the switch has been closed for 3.0 ms, and (d) the energy stored then. (e) The switch opens and the current falls to zero in about 0.10 ms. Estimate the EMF across the primary. (f) A secondary winding on the same iron core has 100 times as many turns. Estimate its EMF.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Time constant. τ = L/R = (6.0 × 10⁻³ H)/(2.0 Ω) = 3.0 × 10⁻³ s = 3.0 ms.
  2. Final current. Once the current is steady the inductor has no voltage across it: Imax = ε/R = 12/2.0 = 6.0 A.
  3. Current after 3.0 ms (= τ). I = 6.0(1 − e⁻¹) = 6.0(0.632) = 3.8 A. Cars cut the switch-on time short like this, especially at high engine speed.
  4. Energy stored. U = ½LI² = ½(6.0 × 10⁻³)(3.8)² = 4.3 × 10⁻² J (43 mJ).
  5. Switch opens. The current tries to drop from 3.8 A to zero in 0.10 ms. The inductor fights that: |ε| = L|ΔI|/Δt = (6.0 × 10⁻³)(3.8)/(1.0 × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 230 V, far more than the battery's 12 V.
  6. The secondary. The same changing flux passes through every turn of both windings, and Faraday's law gives each turn the same EMF. With 100 times the turns, the secondary's EMF is about 100 times larger: ≈ 23 kV, enough to jump the spark plug's gap. The stored 43 mJ is what powers the spark.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the inductor has no effect: it only matters while the current is changing.
  • Using τ = RL instead of L/R.
  • Thinking the current starts at ε/R. It starts at zero when the switch closes (an inductor opposes the change).
  • Forgetting that opening a switch in an inductive circuit can create a huge voltage: with dI/dt very large, the back-EMF L dI/dt can spark across the switch.

Inductance

  • Definition: L = NΦB/I (flux linkage per amp). For a long solenoid of length ℓ, area A and N turns: L = μ0N²A/ℓ = μ0n²Aℓ.
  • Back-EMF: εL = −L dI/dt: it points so as to oppose the change in current.
  • Energy: U = ½LI², stored as magnetic field with energy density u = B²/(2μ0).

The LR circuit

  • Rising: loop rule ε − IR − L dI/dt = 0, giving I = (ε/R)(1 − e−t/τ), τ = L/R.
  • Decaying: I = I0e−t/τ.
  • Two limits: at t = 0 an inductor acts like an open switch (current can't jump); at t → ∞ it acts like a plain wire. (A capacitor is the opposite.)

LR circuits with more than one resistor

  • Use the two limits: just after a switch changes, the inductor keeps its current; a long time later it acts as a plain wire and the current settles by ordinary series and parallel rules.
  • The time constant is τ = L/Req, where Req is the resistance the inductor "sees" across its own terminals.
  • Then I(t) = I∞ + (I0 − I∞)e−t/τ, with I₀ the current just after the change.
  • Opening a switch with the inductor still carrying current forces that current through whatever is left: a large resistor makes a large voltage spike, I0R.
