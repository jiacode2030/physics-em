Charge & Coulomb's law

Charge comes in two kinds. Like charges push apart, opposite charges pull together, and the push or pull falls off with the square of the distance, exactly like gravity does, but trillions of times stronger.

The intuition

Rub a balloon on your hair and it sticks to the wall. Rubbing moved electrons from one surface to the other, so one surface is left slightly negative and the other slightly positive. Charge is never created or destroyed, only moved. Every object is made of huge numbers of + and − charges that almost perfectly cancel.

Coulomb's law says how hard two small charges push or pull: double one charge and the force doubles; double the distance and the force drops to a quarter. It has the same shape as Newton's gravity law, with charge playing the role of mass. The big difference is that gravity only attracts, while electric forces can attract or repel, and they are enormously stronger: the electric force between a proton and an electron in hydrogen is about 10³⁹ times the gravitational pull between them.

When there are three or more charges, the force on any one is found by superposition: work out the force from each of the others as if it were alone, then add the arrows as vectors.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Start with a +3 and a −2 μC pair and watch both arrows. Then make them both positive.

    What you should see

    Opposite charges attract: the arrows point toward each other. Same-sign charges repel, so the arrows flip to point apart. The two arrows are always the same length (Newton's third law).

  2. Drag charge 2 to about twice as far from charge 1 as it was. Read the force before and after.

    What you should see

    Double the distance and the force drops to one quarter. The readout "Force if r doubled" predicts it before you move.

  3. Switch to three charges. Make charges 2 and 3 equal and place charge 1 midway between them.

    What you should see

    Equal pushes from opposite sides cancel, so the net force on charge 1 is zero. Superposition works on arrows, not just numbers.

  4. Double the size of charge 2 (−2 μC to −4 μC) and compare the force readout.

    What you should see

    Doubling either charge doubles the force. The force is proportional to the product q₁q₂.

Worked example

A +2.0 μC charge sits at x = 0, a −3.0 μC charge at x = 0.20 m and a +4.0 μC charge at x = 0.40 m. Find the net electric force on the −3.0 μC charge.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Draw the arrows first. The −3.0 μC charge is attracted to both positive charges. The +2.0 μC charge is to its left, so that force points left (−x). The +4.0 μC charge is to its right, so that force points right (+x).
  2. Force from the +2.0 μC charge (r = 0.20 m). F1 = (8.99 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)(3.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.20)² = 1.35 N, pointing −x.
  3. Force from the +4.0 μC charge (r = 0.20 m). F3 = (8.99 × 10⁹)(4.0 × 10⁻⁶)(3.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.20)² = 2.70 N, pointing +x.
  4. Add the vectors. Fnet = +2.70 − 1.35 = +1.35 N, so the net force is 1.35 N in the +x direction.
  5. Check. The stronger pull comes from the bigger charge, which is on the right, so the net force points right. Both charges are the same distance away, so the net is just k(3.0 μC)(4.0 − 2.0 μC)/(0.20)² = 1.35 N ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Using the signs of the charges in the formula and choosing the direction from the picture, which counts the sign twice. Use |q₁q₂| for the size and the sketch for the direction.
  • Adding force magnitudes when the forces point in different directions. Add components.
  • Putting charge in microcoulombs or distance in centimeters straight into the formula. Convert to C and m.
  • Forgetting that the force is on one charge from the others. A charge never feels a force from itself.

What the law says

  • Direction: along the line joining the charges. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
  • Size: proportional to each charge and to 1/r². Double the distance, a quarter of the force; triple it, a ninth.
  • Newton's third law: the force on charge 1 from charge 2 is equal and opposite to the force on 2 from 1, even if the charges are very different.
  • Charge is quantized: it comes in whole multiples of e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. One electron carries −e, one proton +e. A coulomb is a huge charge: about 6 × 10¹⁸ electrons.
  • Conservation of charge: the total never changes. If two identical conducting spheres touch, they share charge equally.

Conductors and insulators

  • Conductors (metals) let charge move freely; insulators (rubber, glass) hold it in place.
  • A charged rod can pull on a neutral object by polarization: charges inside shift slightly so that the nearer charge, which is opposite, attracts more than the farther one repels.

Using it with gravity-sized numbers

  • Electric forces dominate for atoms and molecules; gravity dominates for planets only because large objects are almost perfectly neutral.
  • The same 1/r² shape means a lot of what you learned about gravity carries over, including adding forces as vectors and the idea of a field.
