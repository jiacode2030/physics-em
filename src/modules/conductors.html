Conductors

In a metal, charges can move freely. So when the field is not zero inside, charges keep moving until it is. That single fact explains shielding, why lightning rods work, and why a charged metal ball keeps all its charge on its outside.

The intuition

A conductor has charges that move freely. Place it in an electric field and the free charges slide: positives one way, negatives the other. They keep moving until the field they create exactly cancels the applied field inside the metal. After that, nothing moves: electrostatic equilibrium. The result: E = 0 everywhere inside a conductor.

Because E = 0 inside, any extra charge must sit on the surface (Gauss's law: a surface inside the metal encloses no net charge). Just outside, the field is perpendicular to the surface with size σ/ε₀. And since E = 0 inside, V cannot change: the whole conductor is at one potential.

This means a hollow metal box shields its inside from outside fields (a Faraday cage), and a charge placed inside a cavity attracts an equal and opposite charge onto the cavity wall, leaving the opposite charge on the outer surface.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Show the sphere in a field. Compare the field inside the sphere to the applied field.

    What you should see

    Inside the metal, E = 0. The field lines stop at the sphere: they end on the induced negative charge on the left and start again from the induced positive charge on the right.

  2. Look at the field at the poles and at the equator.

    What you should see

    At the poles the field is 3 times E₀: lines crowd together. At the equator it is zero. A conductor concentrates field at its pointy bits.

  3. Switch to the shell and put a +3 nC charge in the cavity. Watch the outside field.

    What you should see

    The cavity wall gets −3 nC, so the metal itself has E = 0. The outer surface gets +3 nC, which makes exactly the field a +3 nC point charge would make outside.

  4. Now ground the shell.

    What you should see

    Earth supplies charge to cancel the outer +3 nC. There is no field outside at all. The inside field is unchanged. Grounding a shell hides the charge inside.

Worked example

Why are you safe inside a car in a thunderstorm? Under a thundercloud the electric field near the ground can reach about 1.0 × 10⁴ V/m, pointing up. Treat the car's metal body as a closed conductor. (a) What is the field inside the car? (b) What charge density collects on the roof, and how much charge is on a 3.0 m² roof? (c) Lightning strikes the car. Where does the charge go? (d) Do the rubber tires protect you?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Field inside. In equilibrium the field in the metal is zero; otherwise its free electrons would keep moving. Take a Gaussian surface inside the metal, all the way around the cabin: E = 0 on it, so it encloses no net charge, and with no charge in the cabin the field there is E = 0 too. The outside field ends on charges on the car's outer surface.
  2. Charge density on the roof. Just outside a conductor, Gauss's law with a small pillbox gives E = σ/ε0. So σ = ε0E = (8.85 × 10⁻¹²)(1.0 × 10⁴) = 8.9 × 10⁻⁸ C/m², positive (the field points away from the roof).
  3. Charge on the roof. Q = σA = (8.9 × 10⁻⁸)(3.0) = 2.7 × 10⁻⁷ C (0.27 μC). An equal negative charge gathers on the underside: the car as a whole stays neutral.
  4. The strike. Charge on a conductor lives on its outer surface, and the huge current (around 30 kA) runs over the outside of the metal body and jumps to the ground. The cabin, enclosed by metal, stays nearly field-free.
  5. The tires? No. The bolt has just crossed kilometers of air, which is an insulator. A few centimeters of rubber make no difference. It is the closed metal shell that protects you, so a convertible or a fiberglass car does not.
  6. Check. Units: (C²/(N·m²))(N/C) = C/m² ✓. The field just outside points straight out of the metal, as it must at any conductor's surface. (A car has windows, so the shielding is not perfect, but the metal frame carries the current around you.)

Common mistakes

  • Saying a conductor has no charge inside because charge can't be inside: excess charge sits on the surface, but the metal is full of charges that merely cancel.
  • Thinking E = 0 inside a conductor means E = 0 in the cavity. A charge in the cavity does make a field there.
  • Forgetting that a neutral shell with +q in the cavity has −q on the inner wall and +q on the outer wall.
  • Assuming the surface charge is spread evenly on a non-spherical conductor. It gathers at sharp points.

Properties of a conductor in equilibrium

  • E = 0 everywhere inside the material.
  • Excess charge sits on the outer surface. If there is a cavity with no charge in it, the cavity wall carries no charge either.
  • Field at the surface is perpendicular to it with size σ/ε0.
  • One potential: the whole conductor, including its surface, is an equipotential. Equipotentials hug the surface, and field lines meet it at right angles.
  • Sharp points have a high surface charge density and a strong local field, which is why lightning rods are pointed and why sharp points make sparks.

Shielding and induction

  • Faraday cage: the field inside an empty closed conducting shell is zero, whatever is outside. (Your car in a thunderstorm.)
  • Charge in a cavity: the cavity wall gets −q, the outer surface gets +q (if the shell was neutral and isolated). The outside field depends only on the total charge and the outer shape, not on where the charge is inside.
  • Charging by induction: bring a charged rod near an isolated conductor, ground it briefly, then remove the ground and the rod. The conductor is left charged, with the sign opposite to the rod.

Joining conductors

  • Connect two conductors with a wire and charge flows until they are at the same potential, not until they hold the same charge.
  • For two spheres far apart, kQ1/r1 = kQ2/r2, so charge splits in proportion to radius: Q1/Q2 = r1/r2. The smaller sphere then has the larger surface charge density, σ ∝ 1/r, and the stronger field at its surface.
  • Two identical spheres that touch share their total charge equally.
  • Total charge is conserved: add the charges first, then split them by the equal-potential condition.
