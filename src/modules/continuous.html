Rods, rings & disks

Real charged objects aren't points. Slice a ring or a disk into tiny pieces, let symmetry cancel the sideways parts, and the sum gives the field along the axis. Far away it becomes kQ/z² again, and up close a disk becomes an infinite sheet.

The intuition

Take a ring of charge and a point on its axis. Every slice of the ring is the same distance from the point, so the integral is easy. But the slices pull in different directions: each field points away from its slice, partly along the axis and partly sideways. The sideways parts of opposite slices cancel, leaving only the axial part.

A disk is a stack of rings. Add up the axial fields of all the thin rings from radius 0 to R. The result starts at zero in the center, grows rapidly as you approach, and levels off at σ/2ε₀ right at the surface: exactly the field of an infinite sheet, because to a point very close to it, the disk looks endless.

The test of any such formula: far away (z much larger than R) it must turn into kQ/z². If your answer doesn't, something is wrong.

    What you should see

    At z = 40 cm with R = 3 cm, the disk is indistinguishable from a point charge: the difference readout is below 0.5%.

Worked example

A ring of radius 0.10 m carries a uniform charge of +6.0 nC. Find the electric field on its axis at z = 0.20 m from the center, and compare it with a point charge of the same size at the center.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Slice and use symmetry. Cut the ring into slices dq. Each is the same distance r = √(z² + R²) from P. Sideways components cancel in pairs, so only the axial part dE cos φ survives, with cos φ = z/r.
  2. Add the axial parts. E = ∫ k dq z/r³ = kz/r³ ∫ dq = kQz/(z² + R²)3/2. Everything outside the integral is the same for every slice.
  3. Plug in the numbers. z² + R² = 0.040 + 0.010 = 0.050 m², and 0.0503/2 = 0.01118. So E = (8.99 × 10⁹)(6.0 × 10⁻⁹)(0.20)/0.01118 = 9.6 × 10² N/C, pointing away from the ring along the axis.
  4. Compare with a point charge. kQ/z² = (8.99 × 10⁹)(6.0 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.20)² = 1.35 × 10³ N/C. The ring gives 28% less: at z = 2R it is still not far enough away to look like a point.
  5. Check the limits. z ≫ R gives kQ/z², and z = 0 gives 0 ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Adding the magnitudes of all the dE vectors without cancelling the sideways parts.
  • Using the distance from the center of the ring (z) for r in kdq/r². The distance from each slice is √(z² + R²).
  • Forgetting that for a disk σ = Q/(πR²), not Q/(2πR).
  • Taking the infinite-sheet formula for a small disk far away. That only works when z is much smaller than R.

Building up from the ring

  • Ring: E = kQz/(z² + R²)3/2. Zero at the center, maximum at z = R/√2 where it equals about 0.385 kQ/R².
  • Disk: stack thin rings of radius r and width dr, each with dq = σ·2πr dr. Integrating gives E = (σ/2ε0)[1 − z/√(z² + R²)].
  • Infinite sheet: let R → ∞ and the bracket becomes 1: E = σ/2ε0, the same at every distance. It points away from the sheet on both sides.

Two rules of thumb

  • For a point much farther than the object's size, treat it as a point charge: kQ/z².
  • For a point much closer than the object's size, treat it as an infinite plane or line.
