Charge slices

When charge is spread along a rod, over a ring or through a ball, you can't plug it into kq/r². Cut it into tiny slices that are each small enough to count as point charges, then add them up. That sum is an integral.

The intuition

The recipe is always the same: cut, treat each piece as a point charge, add. A tiny piece of charge is called dq. For a rod, dq = λ dx, where λ is the charge per meter. A slice of a surface has dq = σ dA, and a slice of a volume has dq = ρ dV.

Slide the number of slices up in the simulation. With one slice the answer is a guess. With ten it is close. With forty it matches the exact integral to within a fraction of a percent. An integral is what you get when the slices become infinitely thin.

Two tricks save most of the work: use symmetry to cancel components before you integrate, and choose slices that are all the same distance from the point (a thin ring, for a point on its axis).

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Use one slice, then add more. Watch the error shrink.

    What you should see

    One slice puts all the charge at the middle of the rod, which is just a point-charge guess. Increase the slices and the sum climbs toward the exact value.

  2. Move the point far from the rod (a = 0.5 m).

    What you should see

    Far away the rod looks like a point charge. Even three slices are close, and the "everything at the center" shortcut is within about 10%.

  3. Bring the point close to the rod (a = 0.05 m) with 3 slices.

    What you should see

    Up close, the near end matters most because of the 1/r². Few slices miss that, and the "everything at the center" shortcut is far off.

  4. Switch to the ramp: charge grows toward the far end.

    What you should see

    Most of the charge is far from P, so the field is smaller than for the same total charge spread evenly.

Worked example

A rod lies along the x-axis from x = 0 to L = 0.40 m. Its charge density grows along the rod: λ = bx with b = 5.0 μC/m². Find the total charge, and the electric field at point P on the axis, a = 0.10 m to the left of the rod.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Pick a slice. dq = λ dx = bx dx. It is a distance a + x from P.
  2. Total charge. Q = ∫0L bx dx = bL²/2 = (5.0 × 10⁻⁶)(0.40)²/2 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ C = 0.40 μC.
  3. Field of one slice. dE = k dq/(a + x)² = kb x dx/(a + x)², pointing away from the rod (−x), because the charge is positive. All slices point the same way, so the sizes just add.
  4. Integrate. E = kb ∫0L x dx/(a + x)² = kb [ln((a + L)/a) + a/(a + L) − 1]. (Use x = (a + x) − a to split the integrand.)
  5. Numbers. ln(5) + 0.20 − 1 = 0.809, so E = (8.99 × 10⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁶)(0.809) = 3.6 × 10⁴ N/C, directed in the −x direction.
  6. Sanity check. A point charge of 0.40 μC at the rod's charge-weighted center (0.267 m from the end, 0.367 m from P) would give 2.7 × 10⁴ N/C. The real answer is larger because 1/r² weights the nearer charge more heavily than the average position suggests. ✓

Common mistakes

  • Using a single r for all the slices when they are at different distances.
  • Forgetting the integrand needs x written in terms of the variable (dq = λ dx with λ depending on x).
  • Adding field magnitudes when the slices' arrows point in different directions (rings and arcs). Use symmetry or components.
  • Integrating over the wrong limits: the slice position runs along the object, not from P.

Symmetry saves work

  • For a ring, the sideways parts of the field cancel in pairs, so only the component along the axis survives: add cos θ times each dE.
  • Pick slices that are all the same distance from the point (a ring for a point on its axis) and the integral has one distance in it.
