Biot–Savart law

Coulomb's law builds electric fields from point charges. The Biot–Savart law does the same for magnetism: cut any current into tiny pieces, find the small field each one makes, and add them up. It is the tool when a wire has an awkward shape.

The intuition

Each short piece of wire with current I and length dl makes a tiny magnetic field that circles around it, with strength proportional to I dl and falling as 1/r². Crucially, the direction is not along r̂ (as with a point charge) but perpendicular to both the current and the line to the point: dB ∝ I dl × r̂/r². Use the right-hand rule: thumb along the current, fingers curl the way B goes.

To find the total field of a loop or wire, you add up the dB from all the pieces. Like the charged ring, the slices of a current loop sit at the same distance from a point on the axis, but their fields point in different directions; the sideways parts cancel in pairs and only the axial component survives.

The result for a straight wire is B = μ₀I/(2πa) for a long wire and a bit smaller for a short one. For a circular loop, B = μ₀I/(2R) at the center. The Biot–Savart law always works, while Ampère's law (next module) is a shortcut that only works with symmetry.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Loop: move P to the center of the loop (z = 0).

    What you should see

    At the center every slice is at distance R, perpendicular to the line to P, and every dB points along the axis. B = μ₀I/(2R) is the largest field on the axis.

  2. Cut the loop down to a half circle (arc = 180°).

    What you should see

    The sideways components no longer cancel, so the total field tilts. The sideways readout is no longer zero. Symmetry is what made the full-loop answer so simple.

  3. Switch to a wire segment and increase its length.

    What you should see

    As the wire gets much longer than the distance a, the field approaches the infinite-wire value μ₀I/(2πa), and doesn't grow any more.

  4. Increase the number of slices with the wire segment.

    What you should see

    With two slices the sum is a rough guess. With 36 it matches the exact integral almost perfectly: the integral is what you get when the slices become infinitely thin.

Worked example

A circular loop of radius 5.0 cm carries 8.0 A. Find the magnetic field (a) at its center and (b) on the axis 12 cm from the center. Give directions using the right-hand rule.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Set up one slice. dB = (μ0/4π) I dℓ × r̂/r². For every slice, dl is perpendicular to the line to P, so |dℓ × r̂| = dℓ and each slice is the same distance r = √(R² + z²) from P.
  2. Keep only the axial part. The sideways parts cancel in pairs. The axial component of each dB is dB cos α = dB (R/r). Add up over the loop: ∫ dℓ = 2πR.
  3. Result. B = (μ0I/4π)(R/r³)(2πR) = μ0IR²/(2(R² + z²)3/2).
  4. (a) At the center (z = 0). B = μ0I/(2R) = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(8.0)/(2 × 0.050) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ T = 0.10 mT.
  5. (b) On the axis, z = 12 cm. B = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(8.0)(0.050)²/(2(0.0025 + 0.0144)3/2) = 5.7 × 10⁻⁶ T = 5.7 μT.
  6. Direction and check. Curl the fingers of your right hand along the current; the thumb gives the direction of B on the axis. The field is much weaker at 12 cm than at the center, and it falls off roughly as 1/z³ for large z ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Treating dB like the field of a point charge (along r̂). It is perpendicular to both dl and r̂.
  • Adding the magnitudes of all the dB without cancelling the sideways components.
  • Using the formula for the center when the point is on the axis away from the center.
  • Forgetting that the loop formula needs the radius in meters.

Using the law

  • Direction: the right-hand rule for dℓ × r̂. For a long wire, the field circles the wire (thumb along I, fingers curl the way B goes).
  • Straight wire segment at perpendicular distance a, spanning angles θ₁ and θ₂ from the perpendicular: B = (μ0I/(4πa))(sin θ1 + sin θ2). For an infinitely long wire, both angles → 90° and B = μ0I/(2πa).
  • Circular arc (angle θ) at its center: B = μ0Iθ/(4πR). A full loop (θ = 2π) gives μ₀I/(2R).
  • Loop on its axis, far away: B ≈ μ0μ/(2πz³) with μ = I·(area): the loop acts like a tiny bar magnet (a magnetic dipole).

Two parallel wires

  • Wire 1 makes a field μ0I1/(2πd) at wire 2, which then feels a force per length F/L = μ0I1I2/(2πd). Parallel currents attract; opposite currents repel.

Field of a single moving charge

  • A current is just moving charge, so one charge q moving at velocity v makes B = (μ0/4π) qv × r̂/r² (for speeds much less than c).
  • It is largest in the plane perpendicular to v, and zero straight ahead of or behind the charge.
  • Compare with the electric field of the same charge: B = (v/c²)(E sin θ), a tiny fraction of E at everyday speeds.
