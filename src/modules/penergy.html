Energy of charges

Move a charge through a potential difference and it gains or loses energy: ΔU = qΔV. That one line gives the speed of particles in accelerators, TV tubes and mass spectrometers, and it gives the energy stored in any arrangement of charges.

The intuition

Think of a charge as a ball on the potential hill. Let it fall through a voltage ΔV and it gains kinetic energy qΔV; the potential energy it loses becomes motion. No need to know the field or the path in between: only the voltage difference matters. That is why this is the tool for "speed after being accelerated through…" questions.

Physicists often use a new unit of energy: the electron-volt. One eV is the energy an electron (or any single-charge particle) gains falling through 1 V: 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. A proton accelerated through 500 V gains 500 eV, with no multiplication needed.

For several charges, the energy of the arrangement is the work it took to assemble them: add up kq₁q₂/r for every pair. Positive means you had to push (like charges close together); negative means the system is bound.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Release a proton through 500 V, then an electron through the same voltage.

    What you should see

    Both gain exactly the same energy: 500 eV. The electron is almost 2000 times lighter, so it ends up about 43 times faster.

  2. Try an alpha particle (charge +2e) at 500 V.

    What you should see

    Twice the charge means twice the energy (1000 eV). It is also about 4 times heavier than a proton, so it ends up slower than a proton.

  3. Quadruple the voltage.

    What you should see

    Four times the energy, but only twice the speed: speed goes as √V.

  4. Switch to the charge system. Make two charges +3 nC and +2 nC and the third zero.

    What you should see

    Like charges: positive energy. It took work to push them together, and the energy would be released if they were let go.

Worked example

A proton is released from rest at the positive plate of a parallel-plate accelerator, with a potential difference of 500 V between the plates. Find its kinetic energy and its speed when it reaches the negative plate. Then compare with an electron.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Which principle? The field between the plates is uniform, but you don't need it. Energy conservation links the start and end directly: ΔK + ΔU = 0.
  2. Energy gained. K = qΔV = (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)(500 V) = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ J = 500 eV.
  3. Speed of the proton. ½mv² = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷, so v = √(2(8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷)/(1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)) = 3.1 × 10⁵ m/s.
  4. Electron. The same 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ J goes to a much lighter particle: v = √(2(8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷)/(9.11 × 10⁻³¹)) = 1.3 × 10⁷ m/s. That is 4% of the speed of light, so ignoring relativity is still fine.
  5. Check. Ratio of speeds should be √(mp/me) = √1836 = 43; 1.3 × 10⁷/3.1 × 10⁵ = 43 ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Using the formula ΔU = qΔV with the potential at one point instead of the difference between two.
  • Forgetting that a negative charge gains energy moving toward higher potential.
  • Putting 500 V into the energy equation as 500 J. Energy is qΔV in joules.
  • Using E = ΔV/d to find speed: you don't need E at all for the energy method.

Energy methods for charges

  • Charge in a potential difference: ½mv² = |q|ΔV. Positive charges speed up going from high to low V, negative charges going from low to high.
  • Two point charges: U = kq1q2/r (zero at infinite separation). If they are released and fly apart, each ends up with kinetic energy and Ktotal = U.
  • Three or more: add every pair once. Three charges have three pairs.
  • Escape and closest approach: a charge shot straight at another stops where its kinetic energy has all turned into potential energy.

The electron-volt

  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Atomic energies are a few eV; X-rays are thousands.
  • A particle with charge ne falling through ΔV volts gains nΔV eV.
