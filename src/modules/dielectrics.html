Dielectrics

Slide an insulator between the plates of a capacitor and its capacitance goes up by a factor κ. The insulator's molecules line up against the field and weaken it. The same effect makes insulators attract charged objects, and it is why real capacitors are stuffed with plastic or ceramic.

The intuition

An insulator has no free charges, but its molecules can be stretched or turned: the positive end pulled one way by the field, the negative end the other. This is polarization. Inside the slab the little dipoles line up so that the positive ends point toward the negative plate. Stack them up and you get a layer of negative bound charge on the face next to the positive plate, and positive bound charge next to the negative plate.

These bound charges make their own field, pointing against the field from the plates. The field inside the dielectric drops to E₀/κ, where κ ≥ 1 is the dielectric constant. For the same charge on the plates, a smaller field means a smaller voltage (ΔV = Ed), so C = Q/ΔV grows by a factor κ.

To keep the story straight, always decide first whether the battery is connected. Isolated (Q fixed): ΔV drops by κ, the field drops by κ, the energy drops by κ. Battery connected (ΔV fixed): Q grows by κ, the field in the gap stays at V/d, the energy grows by κ.

The capacitor starts at 100 V before the slab goes in (C₀ = 88.5 pF). Slide the slab in and compare the two modes: with the battery off, the voltage and field fall; with the battery on, the charge and energy rise. The small ± on the slab are bound charges from polarization.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Disconnect the battery (isolated), then push the κ = 4 slab all the way in.

    What you should see

    Capacitance goes up 4×. The charge cannot change, so the voltage drops to 25 V and the field falls to a quarter. The energy also falls to a quarter: the field pulls the slab in.

  2. Do the same with the battery connected.

    What you should see

    The battery holds 100 V. The field stays at V/d, but the charge on the plates rises 4× because the capacitor can hold more at the same voltage. The energy ½CV² rises 4×; the battery paid for it.

  3. Increase κ to 10 with the slab fully in, isolated.

    What you should see

    Voltage drops to 10 V. A material with a large κ is a very strong way to boost capacitance (water κ ≈ 80, some ceramics > 1000).

  4. Push the slab in only halfway.

    What you should see

    The capacitor acts like two capacitors in parallel, one with the slab (κC₀/2) and one without (C₀/2): C = C₀(1 + κ)/2.

Worked example

An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has C₀ = 100 pF and is charged to 200 V, then disconnected from the battery. A dielectric slab with κ = 4 is slid in until it fills the gap. Find the new voltage, the energy before and after, and explain where the energy went. What would happen with the battery still connected?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Charge. Q = C0ΔV0 = (100 pF)(200 V) = 20 nC, and it stays 20 nC because the capacitor is isolated.
  2. New capacitance and voltage. C = κC0 = 400 pF so ΔV = Q/C = 50 V. The field also drops to one quarter.
  3. Energy before and after. U0 = ½C0ΔV0² = 2.0 μJ and U = Q²/(2C) = 0.50 μJ. The energy fell by 1.5 μJ.
  4. Where did it go? The field attracts the dielectric (the slab is pulled in). That 1.5 μJ became kinetic energy of the slab, or work done on whoever held it back.
  5. Battery connected instead. The voltage stays 200 V, so Q = κC0ΔV = 80 nC (the battery supplies 60 nC more), and U = ½(400 pF)(200)² = 8.0 μJ. The battery did 12 μJ of work (60 nC × 200 V): 6 μJ went into stored energy and 6 μJ into pulling the slab in.

Common mistakes

  • Using κ as the factor for the field in the wrong direction: E goes down by κ for the same free charge.
  • Not saying whether the battery is connected. Q fixed and V fixed give opposite answers.
  • Treating a dielectric like a conductor. Inside a conductor E = 0; inside a dielectric E = E₀/κ.
  • Forgetting that the energy changes. Something does work as the slab moves.

What a dielectric does

  • Polarization: the field distorts (or rotates) the molecules into small dipoles aligned with the field.
  • Bound charge: the dipoles leave a surface layer of bound charge on each face. Its surface density is σb = σf(1 − 1/κ), where σf is the free charge on the plates.
  • Net field: E = (σf − σb)/ε0 = σf/(κε0) = E0/κ.
  • The permittivity of the material is ε = κε0. All the vacuum formulas work with ε0 replaced by κε0 inside the dielectric.

Why real capacitors use them

  • Higher capacitance for the same size (κ ≈ 2–5 plastics, hundreds to thousands for ceramics).
  • Higher breakdown voltage than air (about 3 × 10⁶ V/m for air), so more charge fits before sparks.
  • They keep the plates apart so they can be very close.
