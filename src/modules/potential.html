Electric potential

Voltage is the energy-per-charge version of the electric field. Instead of arrows to add, you get one number at each point, like the height on a hill. Positive charges slide downhill, negative charges slide uphill, and equal-height lines are called equipotentials.

The intuition

A hiker on a hill feels a force along the steepest downhill direction. Electric potential V works the same way: the field E points downhill on the V map, and its size is the steepness. In symbols, E = −dV/dx. A contour line on a hill map is the line of equal height; an equipotential is a line of equal V, and field lines always cross it at a right angle.

Potential is defined as energy per charge: V = U/q. A 1 V potential difference means every coulomb gains 1 joule when it falls through it. A battery is a device that holds a potential difference between its terminals.

Since V is a number, not an arrow, adding potentials from several charges is just addition, with signs. That makes potential much easier than field for many-charge problems. Find V, then take a slope if you need E.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Show a dipole (+2 and −2 nC). Drag probe A to the + charge, then to the − charge.

    What you should see

    Potential is high (red) near the positive charge, low (violet) near the negative one. It is exactly zero along the line halfway between them.

  2. Put probe A and probe B on the same contour line.

    What you should see

    The potentials are equal, so ΔV = 0 and the field does no work moving a charge between them, however long the path.

  3. Look at the 3D hill for the dipole.

    What you should see

    A + charge is a hill, a − charge is a well. A positive charge placed on the surface would roll downhill toward the well; the steeper the slope, the stronger the field.

  4. Make charges 1 and 2 both +2 nC.

    What you should see

    Two hills. Between them there is a saddle where V is not zero but E is: V is the sum of two positive numbers, while the fields cancel.

Worked example

A +4.0 nC charge is at x = 0 and a −2.0 nC charge is at x = 0.30 m. (a) Find the electric potential at the point P on the x-axis at x = 0.10 m. (b) How much work must an outside force do to bring a +1.0 nC charge slowly from infinity to P?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Set up. Potential is a scalar, so add the contribution of each charge with its sign: V = kq1/r1 + kq2/r2. The distances are 0.10 m and 0.20 m. There is nothing to resolve into components.
  2. Potential from each charge. V1 = (8.99 × 10⁹)(4.0 × 10⁻⁹)/0.10 = +360 V and V2 = (8.99 × 10⁹)(−2.0 × 10⁻⁹)/0.20 = −90 V.
  3. Add. VP = 360 − 90 = +270 V. P is on the positive side overall.
  4. Work to bring a charge from infinity. The outside force does work equal to the change in potential energy: W = qΔV = (1.0 × 10⁻⁹ C)(270 V − 0) = 2.7 × 10⁻⁷ J. It is positive because the +4 nC charge pushes the +1 nC charge back.
  5. Check. The nearby +4 nC charge dominates (0.10 m away), so the potential should be positive and a bit less than 360 V ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Adding potentials as vectors, or using the vector sum of fields. V is a scalar.
  • Dropping signs: a negative charge contributes a negative number to V.
  • Saying E = 0 wherever V = 0 (or V = 0 wherever E = 0). They are different things: E is the slope of V, not its value.
  • Mixing up energy and potential: U = qV. A 1 V difference gives an electron 1 eV of energy, not 1 J.

Potential and field

  • E points downhill (toward lower V), perpendicular to equipotentials. Where contour lines crowd together, V changes fast and the field is strong.
  • In one dimension: Ex = −dV/dx. In 3D, E = −∇V (the three partial derivatives).
  • Uniform field: V changes linearly: ΔV = −Ed over a distance d along E. The potential is higher on the side the field points away from.
  • Zero field does not mean zero potential. Inside a conductor E = 0 but V is the same nonzero constant everywhere.

Why equipotentials matter

  • No work is done moving a charge along an equipotential (ΔV = 0).
  • A conductor in equilibrium is an equipotential, so field lines hit its surface at right angles.
  • The potential of a point charge is kq/r, not 1/r²: only one power of r in the denominator.

Potential of a continuous charge distribution

  • Because V is a scalar, the integral is easier than for E: V = ∫ k dq/r, with no components and no cancelling. Slice the charge, find each slice's distance to the point, add.
  • Ring, on its axis: every slice is the same distance, so V = kQ/√(z² + R²).
  • Disk, on its axis: stack rings: V = (σ/2ε0)(√(z² + R²) − z).
  • Spherical shell: outside, kQ/r; inside, the potential is constant, kQ/R (E = 0 inside, so V cannot change).
  • Solid uniform sphere: outside kQ/r; inside kQ(3R² − r²)/(2R³), so the center is at 3kQ/(2R).
  • From E to V: V(b) − V(a) = −∫ab E · dr. Work inward from the zero of potential (usually infinity) and integrate piece by piece when E changes form inside and outside an object.
