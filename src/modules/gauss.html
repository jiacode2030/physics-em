Flux & Gauss's law

Gauss's law is Coulomb's law in a more powerful costume. Draw an imaginary closed surface, count the net field passing through it, and the answer depends only on the charge inside. When the charge has symmetry, it gives the field in a single line.

The intuition

Imagine field lines as water streaming out of every positive charge. Put a closed net around some charges and count the water flowing through it. The count only depends on how many positive sources are inside, minus how many negative sinks. Move the charges around inside, change the shape of the net, even add charges outside: the net count doesn't change.

That count is the electric flux, Φ. Gauss's law says Φ equals the enclosed charge divided by ε₀. In the simulation the "measured flux" adds up E·n̂ over the whole sphere; it always matches qenc/ε₀, however the charges are arranged.

The law is always true. It is only useful for finding E when the field is the same everywhere on your surface (or zero, or parallel to it). That needs symmetry: spherical, cylindrical or planar. Then ∮E·dA becomes just E times an area, and you can solve for E.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Start with only charge 1 inside the sphere. Then slide the sphere radius so charge 2 moves from inside to outside.

    What you should see

    Each time a charge crosses the surface, the enclosed charge changes and so does the flux. The measured and predicted flux agree every time.

  2. Cancel everything: charge 1 = +3, charge 2 = −3 (both inside).

    What you should see

    The enclosed charge is zero, so the net flux is zero, even though the field at the surface is certainly not zero. Field lines that go out also come back in.

  3. Switch to the profile for a solid sphere and drag r from inside to outside.

    What you should see

    Inside, E grows linearly with r (only the charge within r counts). Outside it falls as 1/r², as if all the charge were at the center. At r = R the two meet.

  4. Switch to the thin shell and look inside.

    What you should see

    A Gaussian sphere inside a shell encloses no charge, so E = 0 there. Outside, the shell looks like a point charge.

Worked example

A solid sphere of radius R = 5.0 cm carries a uniform charge Q = +8.0 nC throughout its volume. Use Gauss's law to find the field at r = 3.0 cm and at r = 10.0 cm.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Use the symmetry. By spherical symmetry E points radially and has the same size at every point of a sphere centered on the charge. So choose a spherical Gaussian surface of radius r, and then Φ = E(4πr²).
  2. Inside (r = 3.0 cm). The charge is spread evenly, so the enclosed charge is the fraction of volume inside r: qenc = Q(r/R)³ = 8.0 nC × (0.60)³ = 1.73 nC.
  3. Solve for E inside. E = qenc/(4πε0r²) = kQr/R³ = (8.99 × 10⁹)(8.0 × 10⁻⁹)(0.030)/(0.050)³ = 1.7 × 10⁴ N/C, pointing outward.
  4. Outside (r = 10.0 cm). All the charge is enclosed, so E = kQ/r² = (8.99 × 10⁹)(8.0 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.10)² = 7.2 × 10³ N/C, exactly like a point charge.
  5. Check at the surface. At r = R both formulas must agree: kQ/R² = kQR/R³ = 2.9 × 10⁴ N/C ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Using Gauss's law to find E when there is no symmetry. The law still holds, but you can't pull E out of the integral.
  • Counting charge outside the surface. Only enclosed charge appears in the law (the outside charges do affect E, but their flux cancels).
  • For a non-conducting sphere, using the full Q inside the sphere instead of the fraction Q(r/R)³.
  • Thinking E is zero everywhere when the flux is zero. Zero net flux just means as much field leaves as enters.

What flux means

  • For a flat surface in a uniform field, Φ = EA cos θ, where θ is the angle between E and the surface's normal (the line sticking straight out of it). Face-on: maximum. Edge-on: zero.
  • For a curved surface or a non-uniform field, chop the surface into little patches, add up E · dA for each, and the sum is the integral ∮ E · dA. The circle on the integral means the surface is closed.
  • Outward flux counts positive, inward flux negative.

Why it is true

  • For a single point charge the flux through a sphere is E·4πr² = kq·4π = q/ε0, independent of r: the 1/r² in the field cancels the r² in the area. Superposition and the shape-independence of the line count extend it to everything.
