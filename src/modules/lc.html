LC oscillations

Connect a charged capacitor to an inductor and the charge sloshes back and forth: the capacitor empties through the coil, the coil's inertia keeps the current going, and it recharges the capacitor the other way. Mass on a spring, in electrical form.

The intuition

A charged capacitor is like a stretched spring: it holds energy ready to push. Connect it to an inductor and the charge starts to flow, but the inductor resists any sudden change in current, so the current builds up gradually. When the capacitor is empty, the current is at its peak: all the energy is in the inductor's magnetic field. The current can't stop instantly either, so it keeps going and charges the capacitor the other way.

Energy sloshes between UE = q²/(2C) and UB = ½LI², with the sum constant if there is no resistance. The frequency is ω₀ = 1/√(LC): a bigger L (more inertia) or a bigger C (a softer spring) slows the oscillation.

Any real circuit has some resistance, which burns energy as heat and makes the oscillation fade: a damped oscillation exactly like the one you met in Mechanics. Radios use LC circuits as tuners: adjust C to select the frequency you want.

The capacitor starts fully charged. The charge flows through the inductor, which makes it keep flowing after the capacitor is empty and charges it the other way. Energy sloshes between the electric field (capacitor) and the magnetic field (inductor), exactly like a mass on a spring. Add damping (a resistor) and the oscillation dies away. Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Run the ideal circuit. Watch the energy bars.

    What you should see

    When q is at its maximum, I is zero and all the energy is electric. A quarter period later the capacitor is empty, I is at its peak, and all the energy is magnetic. The total never changes.

  2. Quadruple the capacitance.

    What you should see

    ω₀ = 1/√(LC) drops by a factor of 2, so the period doubles. Four times the C means four times the storage for charge at the same voltage, but the circuit takes longer to swing.

  3. Add some damping (0.3).

    What you should see

    The resistor turns energy into heat each cycle, so the amplitude falls exponentially: the envelope is e^(−Rt/2L).

  4. Set damping to 1.0 (critical damping).

    What you should see

    No oscillation at all: the charge flows away and settles to zero as fast as possible without overshooting. Beyond that (overdamped) it decays more slowly.

Worked example

A 20 μF capacitor is charged to 10 V and then connected across a 50 mH inductor (no resistance). Find (a) the angular frequency, frequency and period, (b) the peak current, and (c) the charge on the capacitor at the instant the current is 0.10 A.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Frequency. ω0 = 1/√(LC) = 1/√((0.050)(20 × 10⁻⁶)) = 1/√(1.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 1000 rad/s, so f = ω0/2π = 159 Hz and T = 2π/ω0 = 6.3 ms.
  2. Total energy and charge. Q0 = CV0 = (20 × 10⁻⁶)(10) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ C, and U = ½CV0² = ½(20 × 10⁻⁶)(100) = 1.0 mJ.
  3. Peak current. When all the energy is in the inductor: ½LImax² = 1.0 mJ, so Imax = √(2(1.0 × 10⁻³)/0.050) = 0.20 A. (Also ω₀Q₀ = (1000)(2.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 0.20 A ✓.)
  4. Charge when I = 0.10 A. Energy in the inductor: ½(0.050)(0.10)² = 0.25 mJ. The rest is in the capacitor: q²/(2C) = 0.75 mJ, so q = √(2(20 × 10⁻⁶)(0.75 × 10⁻³)) = 1.7 × 10⁻⁴ C.
  5. Check. q = Q0√(0.75) = 0.866Q0 = 1.7 × 10⁻⁴ C ✓: when the current is half its peak, the charge is 87% of its peak, not half.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that the maximum current and the maximum charge are never at the same time.
  • Using f = 1/√(LC) (forgetting the 2π) or ω₀ = √(LC).
  • Thinking energy is lost in an ideal LC circuit. It is only transferred between the electric and magnetic fields.
  • Mixing up the analogy: L plays the role of mass and 1/C the role of the spring constant (not the other way round).

Adding resistance (RLC)

  • Loop rule: L d²q/dt² + R dq/dt + q/C = 0.
  • Underdamped (small R): q ≈ Q0e−Rt/2L cos ω′t, with ω′ = √(ω₀² − (R/2L)²).
  • Critical damping at R = 2√(L/C); beyond that, overdamped.
