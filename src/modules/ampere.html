Ampère's law

Gauss's law finds E when there is symmetry. Ampère's law does the same for B: choose a loop, add up B along it, and the answer depends only on the current passing through the loop. Wires, solenoids and toroids each take one line.

The intuition

Walk around a closed loop and add up the part of B that points along your path, times each little step. Ampère's law says this total equals μ₀ times the current that pierces the loop. It is not saying B depends only on that current, but that the loop sum does. When symmetry makes B constant and along the path, the sum is just B times the length of the loop, and you can solve for B immediately.

For a long straight wire the field circles the wire, so a circular loop is the natural choice: B(2πr) = μ₀I. For a thick wire with current spread evenly, a loop inside the wire encloses only the fraction of the current within it: B grows linearly from zero at the center and then falls off as 1/r outside.

A long solenoid packs the wire into a coil. A rectangular loop with one side inside and one outside shows that the field inside is uniform, B = μ₀nI, and nearly zero outside. A toroid (a solenoid bent into a doughnut) has B = μ₀NI/(2πr) inside the doughnut and zero outside.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Thick wire: move the Amperian loop from inside the wire to outside.

    What you should see

    Inside, B grows in proportion to r because only the current within the loop counts. At r = R it peaks, and outside it falls as 1/r.

  2. Double the current in the thick wire.

    What you should see

    The field at every point doubles. B is proportional to I at fixed r.

  3. Solenoid: move the loop outside the coil.

    What you should see

    Outside an ideal long solenoid the field is zero. The inside field μ₀nI is uniform; it doesn't depend on where you are inside, or on the coil's radius.

  4. Toroid: look at the field inside the doughnut and just outside it.

    What you should see

    Inside the doughnut the field falls as 1/r (stronger on the inner edge). Everywhere else, including the hole in the middle, it is zero.

Worked example

A long straight wire of radius R = 2.0 cm carries a total current of 12 A, uniformly distributed over its cross-section. Find the magnetic field at r = 1.0 cm and at r = 6.0 cm from the axis.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Choose the loop. By symmetry B circles the axis and has the same size everywhere on a circle centered on the wire. Choose a circular Amperian loop of radius r. Then ∮ B · dℓ = B(2πr).
  2. r = 1.0 cm (inside). The current enclosed is the fraction of the area: Ienc = I(r/R)² = 12 × (0.50)² = 3.0 A. Then B = μ0Ienc/(2πr) = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(3.0)/(2π × 0.010) = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ T.
  3. r = 6.0 cm (outside). All the current is enclosed: B = μ0I/(2πr) = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(12)/(2π × 0.060) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T.
  4. Check at the surface (r = R). Both formulas must agree: μ0I/(2πR) = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ T, the maximum field. At 1 cm it is half the value at the surface (r/R), 60 μT ✓.
  5. Direction. Curl your fingers around the wire so that your thumb points along the current: B circles in the direction your fingers curl. Out of the page gives counterclockwise field lines.

Common mistakes

  • Using the total current for a loop inside a thick wire. Only the current inside the loop counts.
  • Picking a loop with no symmetry. The law is always true, but you can't solve for B without a loop where B is constant and along the path.
  • Using n as the total number of turns for a solenoid. n is turns per unit length.
  • Saying B = 0 outside a toroid 'because there is no current there'. It is zero because an Amperian loop out there encloses no net current (the wire passes through it in both directions).

How it fits with the others

  • Ampère's law is to magnetic fields what Gauss's law is to electric fields: a way to get the field quickly when symmetry allows it. Biot–Savart is the always-works version (like Coulomb's law).
  • The law as written works for steady currents. For changing electric fields, Maxwell added a term (the displacement current), which comes up in the last module.
