How the free-response section works

Four questions, 100 minutes, half your score. One question of each type:

Mathematical RoutinesDerive and calculate. Expect separation of variables, integrals and "in terms of M, k and physical constants."
Translation Between RepresentationsMove between graphs, equations, diagrams and words, and explain why they agree.
Experimental Design and AnalysisPlan a lab, linearize data, find a slope and explain errors.
Qualitative/Quantitative TranslationMake a prediction in words first, then derive, then connect the two.

How to earn points

  • Start from a fundamental equation (ΣF = ma, energy conservation, a definition) before substituting.
  • "Derive" means show the steps symbolically. Write the answer in terms of the given symbols, not numbers.
  • Free-body diagrams: one arrow per force, each starting on the object, labeled, pointing the right way. No "ma" arrow.
  • "Justify" or "explain" needs physics reasoning, ideally pointing back to an equation or principle. A correct choice with no reason usually earns nothing.
  • Later parts are often graded against your earlier answers. If (b) is wrong but you use it correctly in (c), you can still earn (c)'s points, so keep going.
  • Units on every number, and a sensible number of significant figures.

Question 1: A puck that slows down

A hockey puck of mass M slides on a horizontal surface. Friction with the surface is negligible, but the puck feels a resistive force F = −kv, where k is a positive constant. At t = 0 the puck is at x = 0 moving in the +x direction with speed v0.

(a) Write, but do not solve, a differential equation for the puck's velocity v(t).

Scoring guide (1 point)

Model answer. M dv/dt = −kv.

(b) Derive an expression for v(t) in terms of M, k, v0, t and physical constants.

Scoring guide (3 points)

Model answer. ∫v₀v dv/v = −(k/M)∫0t dt gives v = v0e−kt/M.

(c) Derive an expression for the total distance the puck travels as t → ∞.

Scoring guide (3 points)

Model answer. x = ∫0∞ v0e−kt/M dt = Mv0/k. The puck never quite stops, yet it covers a finite distance.

(d) Calculate the work done on the puck by the resistive force from t = 0 until it effectively stops. Explain how this is consistent with your answer to (b).

Scoring guide (2 points)

Model answer. W = ΔK = 0 − ½Mv0². Since v → 0 in (b), the force eventually removes all the kinetic energy.

(e) A second puck of mass 2M is launched with the same speed and the same k. Does it travel farther than, less far than, or the same distance as the first puck? Justify using your answer to (c).

Scoring guide (1 point)

Model answer. Farther, twice as far. The distance is proportional to M: the heavier puck has more momentum for the same drag to remove.

Question 2: A cart pushed by a changing force

A 2.0 kg cart starts at rest on a level, frictionless track. The graph shows the net horizontal force on it from t = 0 to t = 6 s.

(a) Sketch the cart's velocity as a function of time from 0 to 6 s. Pay attention to the shape (curved or straight) in each interval.

Scoring guide (3 points)

Model answer. The slope of v–t is the acceleration, F/m. Where F grows, the slope grows; where F is constant, the line is straight; where F is zero, v is flat.

(b) Calculate the cart's speed at t = 5 s.

Scoring guide (2 points)

Model answer. J = ½(2)(6) + (2)(6) + ½(1)(6) = 21 N·s, so v = J/m = 10.5 m/s.

(c) Derive an expression for v(t) for 0 ≤ t ≤ 2 s.

Scoring guide (2 points)

Model answer. F = 3t, a = 1.5t, v = ∫0t 1.5t′ dt′ = 0.75t². At 2 s this gives 3 m/s, matching the triangle area 6 N·s ÷ 2 kg.

(d) Explain why your sketch should have no sharp corner at t = 2 s, referring to your answer to (c).

Scoring guide (2 points)

Model answer. The slope of v–t is the acceleration. Just before 2 s it is 1.5·2 = 3 m/s²; just after it is 6 N / 2 kg = 3 m/s². Equal slopes on both sides mean no corner.

(e) Calculate the work done on the cart by the net force from 0 to 5 s.

Scoring guide (1 point)

Model answer. W = ΔK = ½(2.0)(10.5)² ≈ 110 J.

Question 3: Measuring g with a pendulum

Students want to measure the acceleration due to gravity using a simple pendulum. They have string, a small heavy ball, a meter stick, a stopwatch and a protractor.

Length L (m)0.200.400.600.801.00
Period T (s)0.911.261.571.792.02
g from your slope—
Fit quality—

(a) Describe a procedure the students could use to collect the data needed. Include what they measure, how, and one step that reduces uncertainty.

Scoring guide (3 points)

Model answer. Hang the ball, measure L to its center, pull it aside less than 15°, release, time 10 full swings and divide by 10. Repeat for five different lengths.

(b) The students record the data above. Indicate which quantities they should graph to produce a straight line whose slope can be used to find g. Explain why.

Scoring guide (2 points)

Model answer. T² = (4π²/g)L, so plot T² on the vertical axis against L.

(c) Plot the linearized data and draw a best-fit line. (Use the interactive graph: adjust the line until it fits.)

Scoring guide (2 points)

Model answer. The points lie close to a line through the origin with slope about 4.06 s²/m.

(d) Use your best-fit line to calculate g.

Scoring guide (2 points)

Model answer. Slope ≈ 4.06 s²/m, so g = 4π²/4.06 ≈ 9.7 m/s².

(e) A different group releases the pendulum from 40° instead. Would their value of g be too large, too small, or about right? Explain.

Scoring guide (1 point)

Model answer. Too small. The small-angle formula underestimates the true period at 40°, so measured periods are longer, the slope is steeper, and 4π²/slope comes out low.

Question 4: Sphere versus hoop

A solid sphere (I = ⅖MR²) and a thin hoop (I = MR²) have the same mass M and radius R. Both are released from rest at the same height H on an incline and roll without slipping.

(a) Without writing equations, predict which object reaches the bottom first. Explain your reasoning in terms of energy.

Scoring guide (3 points)

Model answer. The sphere wins. Both get the same energy from gravity, but the hoop spends more of it spinning, so it has less left for moving down the ramp.

(b) Derive an expression for the hoop's speed at the bottom in terms of H and physical constants.

Scoring guide (3 points)

Model answer. MgH = ½Mv² + ½(MR²)(v/R)² = Mv², so v = √(gH).

(c) Derive an expression for the sphere's speed at the bottom.

Scoring guide (2 points)

Model answer. MgH = ½Mv²(1 + ⅖) = (7/10)Mv², so v = √(10gH/7) ≈ 1.20√(gH).

(d) Explain how your answers to (b) and (c) support your prediction in (a).

Scoring guide (2 points)

Model answer. The sphere is about 20% faster at the bottom, and the same calculation at any height shows it is faster everywhere along the way. The hoop spends 1/2 of its energy spinning; the sphere only 2/7.

Which principle do I use?

Most of the time lost on exams goes into choosing a method. Learn the clues, then practice picking.

The intuition

Each big principle answers a different kind of question. Forces tell you how things accelerate right now. Energy connects "before" and "after" without caring about the path. Momentum handles quick, violent interactions where the forces are unknown. Angular momentum does the same for spinning. Torque balance handles things that stay put.

Many hard problems are two easy problems glued together. Split them at the moment the physics changes (a collision ends, a string goes slack).

Clue in the problemReach for
Asks for a time, or gives a constant accelerationKinematics (plus forces to find a)
Asks for a force: tension, normal force, frictionForces: free-body diagram and ΣF = ma (ΣF = mv²/r on curves)
Asks for a speed after a height or spring change; the path is curvy or unknownEnergy
A collision, explosion, or push-off; a very brief, huge forceMomentum (impulse for the force)
Something spinning changes shape, or something sticks to a pivoted or spinning objectAngular momentum
An extended object at rest with hinges, cables, supportsTorque balance Στ = 0 (and ΣF = 0)
A collision followed by a swing or slideMomentum for the collision, then energy
A force at a specific point on a curved path (top of a loop, bottom of a swing)Energy for the speed, then forces for the force

Practice: pick the principle

Choose the first tool you would reach for.

1. A car brakes from 30 m/s to rest in 4.0 s. How far does it travel?

2. A skier starts from rest at the top of a 40 m frictionless hill of unknown shape. How fast are they going at the bottom?

3. Two skaters at rest push off each other. One moves away at 3 m/s. How fast does the other move?

4. A sled is pulled at constant speed up a rough slope. What is the rope tension?

5. A spinning skater pulls in their arms. What is the new spin rate?

6. A uniform beam is held by a hinge at one end and a cable at the other. What is the cable tension?

7. A bullet embeds in a hanging wooden block. How high does the block swing?

8. A block slides down a rough ramp. How long does it take to reach the bottom?

9. A spring launches a ball straight up. How high does it go?

10. A ball rolls without slipping down a ramp. How fast is it at the bottom?

11. A lump of clay hits and sticks to the end of a rod pivoted at its other end. How fast does the rod spin right after?

12. A hockey puck hits the boards and bounces back. What average force did the boards exert during the 0.01 s contact?

13. A pendulum bob is released from 30°. What is the string tension at the bottom?

14. A satellite's elliptical orbit brings it as close as 7000 km to Earth's center, where it moves at 8.7 km/s. How fast is it at the far point, 14,000 km from the center?

15. A ball is thrown horizontally off a cliff. Where does it land?

16. You push a merry-go-round with a known torque. How long until it reaches 2 rad/s?
